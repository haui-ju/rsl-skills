Accessible Technology for Adults with Down Syndrome: A
Scoping Review

HAILEY L. JOHNSON and BILGE MUTLU, Department of Computer Sciences, University of
Wisconsin-Madison, Madison, Wisconsin, USA


This article presents a scoping review of assistive technologies designed to support Adults with Down Syndrome
(AwDS), focusing on individuals aged 18 and older. We define assistive technologies as digital tools used
directly by AwDS to assist them in performing a task or achieving a goal. The review aimed to: (1) examine
how researchers have involved AwDS in the design and evaluation of technologies; (2) identify the types of
technologies explored and the motivations driving their use; (3) assess how these technologies support abilities
across life domains; and (4) analyze the reported benefits, challenges, and design implications. Following
PRISMA-ScR guidelines, we searched six academic databases (ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Compendex,
Scopus, ScienceDirect, and SpringerLink), resulting in 1,944 articles. After applying the inclusion criteria, we
identified 20 studies for in-depth analysis. Methods ranged from experimental to participatory design, with
inclusive strategies such as caregiver involvement, visual aids, and personalized supports frequently reported.
Technologies identified included computers and web platforms, mobile applications, AAC systems, smart
home tools, and social robots, supporting areas like daily living, communication, learning, navigation, and
health. Reported benefits included increased independence and digital inclusion, enhanced communication,
expanded social connection, greater confidence and engagement, and enhanced skill development. Common
challenges included interface complexity, lack of context-aware interaction, environmental and sensory
constraints, and uneven impact across users. Based on these findings and paper analysis, we outline five
design recommendations: reduce interaction complexity, support engagement through feedback and flexible
pacing, enable personalization and long-term adaptability, provide accessible pathways to secure system
access, and embed technology within everyday routines. This review emphasizes the importance of inclusive,
user-centered systems that align with the goals, contexts, and lived experiences of AwDS.

1   Introduction
Technology is playing an increasingly central role in everyday life, influencing how we commu-
nicate, learn, work, navigate spaces, and express ourselves. For many, digital tools can enhance
autonomy, productivity, and social connection. Despite its widespread adoption, individuals and
groups within our society face barriers to accessing the benefits of technology, highlighting the
need to design more inclusive technologies for a wider range of people while including people with
disabilities [114, 151]. People with cognitive disabilities, such as Adults with Down Syndrome
(AwDS), may face barriers in accessing mainstream, emerging, or assistive technologies. These
barriers can stem from cost [78, 141], lack of awareness [53, 78, 121], limited training opportunities
[53, 85, 128], or a mismatch between the technology’s design and the user’s preferences or situated
abilities [112, 141, 155]. Whether navigating common technologies, such as smartphones, tablets,
and social media, or using specialized systems, including Augmentative and Alternative Com-
munication (AAC) devices, smart home tools, or immersive environments, AwDS may encounter
technologies that are not tailored to their preferences, contexts, or goals. Understanding how
technologies have been designed for individuals with AwDS and how these individuals experience
them is essential to ensuring more equitable and meaningful access.
   In the United States, AwDS are living longer than ever before, with the mean and median
age of death increasing from just 3 and 0 years in 1950 to 48 and 54 years in 2010 [40]. This
transformation reflects decades of advocacy and reform in healthcare, education, employment,
social services, and disability rights. As a result, people with Down Syndrome (DS) are increasingly
participating in their communities, engaging in employment, continuing education, and building
social relationships across their lifespan [66, 75, 133]. As their participation expands, so must our
efforts to understand and support their interaction with technology, on their terms and in ways
that reflect their identities, goals, and expertise. Historically, the perspectives and lived experiences
of individuals with DS have often been overlooked in research and design, either by grouping
them into broader disability categories or relying solely on caregiver or expert reports. Within
research, the DS community is often grouped within the broader Intellectual and Developmental
Disabilities (IDD) community [92]. Intellectual Disability is defined by the American Association
on IDD as “a condition characterized by significant limitations in both intellectual functioning and
adaptive behavior that originates before the age of 22” [113], where adaptive behaviors are the
collection of conceptual, social, and practical skills that are learned and performed by people in
their everyday lives [113]. Based on this definition, IDD may be non-specific or may encompass
people from a diverse range of conditions and communities, including DS, Autism Spectrum
Disorder (ASD), Cerebral Palsy, Fragile X, Turner syndrome, Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorders,
and so on. While there is overlap between the communities, each are distinct, and people with DS
have unique and variable cognitive, physical, and social characteristics that warrant more specific
attention in research and design [35, 46, 61, 117].
   A growing body of systematic literature reviews has investigated how assistive technology can
support individuals with IDD and DS, particularly in childhood. Boato et al. [19] reviewed virtual
spaces for therapy and their effects on cognitive and motor development in children with DS. Their
review identified games that supported motor coordination [18, 97], visual-motor integration [144,
152], and balance [5] through motion tracking and real-time feedback. They found that children
often responded positively to technologies that included visual rewards, simple interfaces, play-
based experiences, individualized calibration, and repetitive routines. Similarly, Shahid et al. [131]
reviewed technology-enhanced support tools for children with DS and found that such technologies
were used to support a range of abilities, including cognitive, social, physical, health, and autonomy-
related skills. Their analysis highlighted tools that targeted cognition [2, 76], especially literacy

as community participation, employment, or independent living.
   From a broader perspective, researchers have also investigated how technology can be developed
in collaboration with people with IDD. Montoya-Rodríguez et al. [108] explored how Virtual
Reality (VR) and Augmented Reality (AR) systems might be used to support adults with IDD,
particularly in the development of social and communication skills. They reviewed interventions
such as virtual job interview training [25, 26], educational games using avatars [28], and social
scenario simulations [96]. Many of these systems provided immersive, engaging experiences that
allowed participants to safely practice social behaviors, increase confidence, and learn cooperative
strategies. Concluding VR/AR offers a rich, contextual learning environment that can be tailored
to user needs. However, they also noted that few systems were developed specifically with or for
individuals with DS, and most studies reported only short-term outcomes.
   In a review of internet use among individuals with IDD, Glencross et al. [55] examined both
the risks and benefits associated with digital participation. Their findings revealed that much of
the existing literature identifies potential harms, such as online crime or exploitation [80, 95],
emotional distress [31, 120], or internet overuse [94]. Despite these concerns, the review highlighted
evidence that many people with IDD use the internet for social connection [17, 134], learning [80],
independence and self-advocacy [120], and sexual expression [94, 95]. People with IDD reported
feeling included and empowered when participating in online spaces, underscoring the importance
of designing technologies that support safety, agency, and access.
   Morash-Macneil et al. [109] conducted a systematic review focused on employment-related
technologies for individuals with IDD. They found a growing trend toward using mainstream mobile
technologies, such as smartphones and tablets, to support task sequencing, schedule reminders,
navigation, and workplace communication. These tools offered advantages in terms of affordability,
familiarity, and social acceptability [32], and many studies demonstrated improvements in job
performance and independence. However, very few of the reviewed studies specifically reported
on individuals with DS, making it difficult to assess how these benefits apply to that population.
   Boot et al. [21] reviewed barriers to accessing assistive technologies for people with IDD across
multiple contexts. They identified structural and systemic issues, including limited funding [129], in-
adequate caregiver training [38], and a lack of awareness [126]. The review recommended co-design
and advocacy to enhance access and relevance. While they offered broad policy recommendations,
their review did not focus on the unique life transitions and experiences of AwDS.

2.1 DS in the Broader Disability Landscape
DS is part of the broader category of IDD, a diverse group of conditions that affect cognitive function,
adaptive skills, and overall development [6]. Many individuals with DS also have ASD (∼10%–15%)
[43], but it is often diagnosed later than in individuals with ASD alone due to overlapping behavioral
traits and delays in motor and language development that complicate early identification [63, 122,
147]. Although individuals with DS and ASD share some developmental patterns with those with
ASD only, they may present with poorer social communication and more stereotyped behaviors than
peers with DS alone [56, 110]. Adults with DS-only tend to achieve higher levels of independence,
social contact, and functional ability than their ASD-only peers [46].
   Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder is also common among people with DS (∼6%–30%) [43,
46] at a higher rate than the neurotypical population. However, ADHD may be underdiagnosed
in this group, as symptoms often go unrecognized or are misattributed to general developmental
delays. Children with DS and co-occurring ADHD frequently experience greater difficulties with
inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity, sleep, and behavioral regulation than peers with DS alone
[44, 47]. These symptoms can be further complicated by medical conditions common in DS (e.g.,
hearing loss, visual impairments, and sleep apnea), which may mimic or exacerbate ADHD-related
behaviors, making diagnosis and intervention more difficult [44]. Conductive hearing loss is
common, with sensorineural hearing loss becoming more prevalent in adulthood, often leading to
the use of hearing aids, cochlear implants, or sign language [6]. Visual impairments are also more
frequent in individuals with DS, with many requiring glasses or surgical interventions. Alzheimer’s
disease significantly affects AwDS, with cognitive decline rates starting below 10% in individuals in
their 40s, increasing to over 30% in their 50s, reaching 50%–80% for those aged 60–69 years, and 95%
for 70+ [16, 118, 140]. Additionally, depression and anxiety are more common in individuals with DS
compared to the general population [101], often related to communication difficulties and atypical
symptom presentation. Mood disorders and anxiety disorders were found to be less common than
those with IDD [10]. Psychological stressors, such as changes in living arrangements, school or
care transitions, and bereavement, may also contribute to these mental health challenges [137].
Despite the prevalence of these comorbidities, Human–Computer Interaction (HCI) research is
yet to extensively consider them when designing technology for individuals with DS. This may be
due to the limited number of publications in the field. Acknowledging the complexity of DS and the
variety of overlapping conditions it encompasses presents an opportunity to design technologies
that potentially benefit a much broader population, while also highlighting the heterogeneity
among AwDS. Many of the limitations in accessing technology in the DS community overlap with
individuals with IDDs, acquired brain injuries, and aging adults. While the characteristics common
for people with DS differ from those of other communities, overlapping needs, such as cognitive
scaffolding, communication support, and sensory accommodations, are common across populations.
For instance, individuals with traumatic brain injuries may also benefit from decluttered interfaces
and simplified interface navigation to reduce cognitive load, much like AwDS [74, 156]. Aging adults
may experience declines in memory, hearing, vision, or fine motor skills that mirror accessibility
needs within the DS population [9, 23]. Aging AwDS also experience similar age-related declines
earlier in life than neurotypical aging adults [73, 157]. The broader challenges of being stereotyped as
incapable of using technology, experiencing poor interface design, or lacking access to personalized
support apply across these communities. Future researchers should also consider collecting more
detailed demographic data, with opt-out options, and investigate how overlapping conditions could
influence technology access, ensuring that innovations are both inclusive and adaptable across
different populations.

2.2    Societal Barriers in the United States of America for AwDS
Medical research has played a critical role in understanding DS, offering insights into its complexi-
ties. However, much of this research has historically framed disability through an impairment-based
lens, focusing on deficits, leading to shifts in how we discuss disabilities [123]. The social model
of disability provides a contrasting perspective, shifting the focus toward how societal structures
and environments create disabling barriers [57]. Designing for individuals with DS should not be
about “fixing” impairments but about reducing barriers to participation, autonomy, and inclusion.
Legal frameworks, particularly guardianship laws, also limit autonomy for AwDS. Many people
are placed under full or partial guardianship, which removes their legal right to make decisions
about healthcare, finances, or relationships. While intended to protect, these arrangements often
rely on outdated assumptions and overlook individual capabilities. Some adults with disabilities
and their families prefer supported decision-making, which offers guidance without removing
control [149]. However, the US legal system still relies heavily on guardianship, limiting personal
independence, relationships, and financial autonomy [146]. Employment has a significant impact
on the well-being and independence of individuals with AwDS, yet major barriers persist. Limited
access to job-specific training and employment services, such as job search support, restricts skill
development [86, 138]. Working memory challenges may further reduce employment chances
[142]. Bush and Tassé [27] reported a decline in paid community employment, with only 16% of
AwDS in such roles from 2011 to 2013. However, adults who regularly make daily decisions (e.g.,
how to spend their time or money) were more likely to be employed, highlighting the value of
fostering decision-making skills. When informed about vocational opportunities, AwDS can make
more empowered choices [60]. Still, many AwDS remain underemployed, working part-time or
unpaid volunteer roles due to limited access to competitive, integrated positions and inadequate
vocational skill development [33]. Housing and service access present additional challenges. Many
AwDS continue to live with family well into adulthood, at a higher rate than peers with other IDD,
largely due to long waitlists, high costs, and a lack of supported housing options [33]. Even with
legal protections, community-based residential options remain limited, and families often provide
care without sufficient professional support or respite services [24]. These issues are compounded
for families from minority or immigrant backgrounds, who may encounter language barriers,
cultural mismatches, or exclusion from planning processes [24, 64]. Some refugee families report
feeling unheard or disrespected by professionals unfamiliar with their cultural norms around family
roles, disability, or decision-making, leading to delays in support and misalignment in services
[64]. Additionally, the lack of affordable or culturally responsive information systems makes it
harder to navigate disability services or secure supports that reflect diverse needs. Fragmented
services, limited specialist care, and insufficient funding for therapy services create additional
hurdles for AwDS [33, 119]. The lack of appropriate programs and specialized professionals also
limits participation in physical activities [100]. The absence of adequate programs for AwDS is a
frequent barrier, as shown in the literature review by Souto et al. [138]. Leisure activities tend to
occur primarily at home, with external barriers such as lack of nearby facilities, social connections,
and transportation limiting broader community participation [106]. Barriers to public infrastructure
and community organization services are facilitated by a lack of public disability programs and
health services [138]. When compared to adults with ASD, Esbensen et al. [46] found that adults
with ASD differed from AwDS across several domains of adult functioning. Adults with ASD had
more difficulty completing daily living tasks and generally required more assistance, while AwDS
were more likely to perform these tasks independently or with minimal support. Literacy levels
also differed, with AwDS often able to read and write simple sentences, whereas adults with ASD
typically could only manage a few words. Behavior problems were more prevalent among the ASD

2.3 Accessible and Assistive Technology for Children with DS
A broad range of technologies has been applied to address cognitive, communication, and motor
abilities, tailored to the unique abilities of children with DS. More than 80% of children have
started using computers by age six, mostly for learning and entertainment [50]. Many applications
are designed to enhance cognitive skills, including speech [49, 71], numeracy [8], and health
monitoring through mobile devices [82]. Games helped improve memory [2], tongue training
[107], speech abilities [135], and emotional expression using avatars to teach children how to
communicate emotions [67]. VR technologies have also been explored to enhance occupational
therapy in children with DS to improve motor proficiency, visual-integrative abilities, and sensory
integrative functions [41, 152]. Robots and mobile apps have been explored to provide support to
therapists/teachers for designing therapeutic and educational intervention plans for children with
DS [59, 125]. AAC devices were used to teach literacy skills and promote communication [12, 69].
Additionally, TUI, such as multitouch tabletops [42], foster the development of literacy and motor
coordination [65, 76]. Feng et al. [51] highlight how different impairments impact technology use
for children with DS. For example, fine motor limitations can cause input errors, but cognitive
and language difficulties make it difficult to understand and recover from these errors without
assistance. Children struggled with navigation, reading instructions, typing, and troubleshooting,
and many lacked the patience or working memory capacity to follow unclear interface flows. While
features like speech input or icon-based design might seem beneficial, the study warns that such
solutions may backfire when other skill areas (e.g., verbal language or abstract reasoning) are also
affected. Notably, children with DS demonstrate stronger social than cognitive or motor skills,
which creates a mismatch as software that is cognitively accessible may feel too juvenile. At the
same time, more age-appropriate programs often rely on unsupported abilities. These findings
underscore the importance of design strategies that anticipate multichannel limitations, provide
scaffolded support across age groups, and strike a balance between emotional engagement and
cognitive demand.

3.1   Systematic Review Method
In this review, we searched for publications in six databases: ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore,
Compendex, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and SpringerLink. The ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, and
Compendex are technology-focused databases, whereas Scopus, ScienceDirect, and SpringerLink
are all science-focused databases. Due to the multidisciplinary nature of the RQs, which involve
both technology and the population, we selected a combination of technology- and science-focused
databases. The databases were queried in November 2023.

3.2 Search String
We searched all six databases with the search string “Down syndrome” AND adult* AND * technolog*,
inspired by Shahid et al. [131] literature review. This search string contains three elements that all
must be true to include a paper in the search results. The first element is the term “Down syndrome,”
which forces both words in the term to be found together. This limits the chances of finding
publications focused on other populations, such as Fragile X syndrome, Williams syndrome, Turner
syndrome, and others. The second element is the term “adult*,” which includes an asterisk (i.e.,
wildcard search) at the end to accommodate for other forms of the word (e.g., adults, adulthood).
The third element is the term “* technolog*,” where “technolog” was used with an asterisk before
the term to retrieve results of various types of technology (e.g., assistive technology, accessible
technology, support technology), and an asterisk at the end to accommodate for other forms of
the word (e.g., technologies, technological). For databases that don’t allow wildcard searches (e.g.,
IEEE and ScienceDirect) because they automatically include searches with various forms of the
three terms, we updated the search string to “Down syndrome” AND adult AND technology. No year
limitation was made during the search process. All limitations are identified in Figure 2 and Table 1.

3.3   Inclusion/Exclusion
Our inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined in Table 1. Publications were included if they: (1)
involved participants with DS aged 18 or older, (2) focused on digital and assistive technologies
directly used by AwDS, (3) reported on outcomes such as study methods, technology design,
conceptual frameworks, technology usability and learnability, or areas of life that technology
aimed to support, and (4) were published in peer-reviewed journals or conference proceedings. We
excluded studies that focused on non-digital interventions, described only technical development
without participant interaction, or did not specify participants as AwDS. Non-English articles, book
chapters, abstracts, and duplicates were also excluded. Two independent reviewers participated
in the screening process for this review. Titles and abstracts were screened separately by both
reviewers using the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Articles that appeared to meet the criteria
or required further evaluation were advanced to full-text review, with an interrater reliability of
0.78. Full-text articles were again assessed independently by both reviewers. Any discrepancies or
conflicts in the inclusion and exclusion decisions were resolved through discussion and consensus,
ensuring consistency and minimizing bias in the selection process. The final inclusion count was
20 publications, with an interrater reliability of 0.90.

they focused on the opinions of stakeholders and experts. We encourage future researchers to
highlight their participants’ perspectives and respectfully include them in research processes. We
also encourage researchers to include AwDS in the consent process, even if some individuals require
consent through a legal authority. We noticed a few observational and ethnographic studies, which
is surprising given their flexibility and potential to capture natural interactions, especially when
participants prefer to demonstrate rather than describe their use of technology. However, within
studies using qualitative or mixed-methods approaches, observation was a common data collection
element. We also observed a lack of participatory design methods, which may be due to the multi-

broader societal challenges [11]. Participatory methods, such as those conducted with focus groups
by Lazar et al. [92], may be particularly useful when working with the DS community. However,
researchers must consider the time and effort required from participants when using participatory
methods. Additionally, expanding the use of ethnographic, longitudinal, or diary-based methods

7   Future Work
Future research should continue to center AwDS in designing, evaluating, and developing tech-
nologies. This means moving beyond broad accessibility claims to create solutions developed in
collaboration with the community that reflect not only what technologies should do, but also
why they matter. Future studies should explore diverse motivations for technology use, such as
personalization, digital autonomy, engagement, and identity expression, and prioritize those that
align with users’ everyday goals. Researchers should also examine the contexts in which support,
whether through technology or people, is welcomed or intrusive. For instance, an AwDS user may
rely on assistive tools to read text messages, but still wants privacy when receiving messages from
a significant other while around family.
   Geographic representation also emerged as a notable limitation. Most reviewed studies were
conducted in North America and Europe, with no included papers from regions such as South
America, Africa, or Asia (excluding one publication from Iran). This lack of cultural diversity limits
the generalizability of findings. It raises questions about how social norms, infrastructure, and local
values influence access to and the design of technology for AwDS. Future work should prioritize
more inclusive sampling, localized research approaches, and culturally sensitive design practices
that reflect global diversity in lived experience and technological contexts. More work could also
be done on the complex contextual relationship between AwDS and their support systems, as well
as on how technology can be used independently or as a support mechanism between groups.
   One critical direction is enhancing personalization. Many current technologies offer limited
ability to adjust difficulty, pacing, or interface elements to match the diverse cognitive and motor
profiles of AwDS. Future systems should offer layered levels of support, enabling users to toggle
between guided and independent modes or adjust complexity over time. Tools such as large
language models and generative AI present new opportunities for real-time adaptation. For example,
personalized avatars, simplified explanations, or dynamically generated task instructions could
help tailor experiences to individual users while preserving autonomy and engagement. AI-driven
feedback loops could further adjust systems based on user progress, offering gentle guidance or
encouragement aligned with user goals.

databases we did not search or if our broad search terms still failed to capture all applicable literature.

9    Conclusion
This scoping literature review synthesized findings from 20 peer-reviewed publications that ex-
amined technologies designed and used by AwDS. The goal of this review was to gain a deeper
understanding of how technology is being developed and studied with this population. We explored
(1) how researchers have involved AwDS in the design and evaluation of technologies, including
inclusive methods and accessibility strategies; (2) the technologies that have been explored and
the motivations and perceived advantages driving their use; (3) how digital technologies have
been applied to enhance abilities in various life domains; and (4) the reported benefits and chal-
lenges experienced by AwDS when interacting with these technologies. Our analysis identified
five key benefits: confidence, enjoyment, and engagement; independence and digital inclusion;
skill development and learning; communication through writing, typing, and verbal expression;
and social inclusion and connection. We also identified four common challenges: interface and
interaction complexity; contextual misalignment in technology use; limited generalization and
uneven impact; and environmental and sensory constraints. We additionally outlined five design
recommendations that emphasize the importance of: (1) reducing interaction complexity through
clear visuals and simplified choices; (2) supporting engagement through responsive feedback and
user-controlled pacing; (3) embedding design into everyday routines and environmental contexts;
(4) designing for individualization, adaptability, and growth over time; and (5) Provide flexible,
user-friendly pathways to secure system access. These insights underscore the need for inclusive,
adaptive, and context-aware technologies that align with the preferences, abilities, and goals of
AwDS. By centering their lived experiences and capabilities, future research and design efforts can
advance toward more meaningful, empowering, and equitable use of technology.
