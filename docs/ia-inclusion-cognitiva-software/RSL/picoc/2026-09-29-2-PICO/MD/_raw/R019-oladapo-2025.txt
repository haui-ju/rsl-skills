1.0. Introduction

The World Report on Disability [1], jointly presented by the World Health Organization (WHO)
and the World Bank, reveals that 15% of the global population, approximately 1 billion people,
experience some form of disability. This number continues to rise due to various factors such as
road accidents, aging populations, and disease outbreaks, which often lead to physical impairments.
Among this demographic, individuals with physical disabilities frequently face challenges in
accessing essential services independently, including academic resources.
In educational institutions, students with physical disabilities often encounter difficulties in
accessing their academic records without third-party assistance. Adekunle Ajasin University,
Akungba Akoko, Ondo State, Nigeria, addresses result processing through the Ajasin Varsity
Examination Result System (AVERS), a web-based solution developed using the Laravel PHP
framework. AVERS allows staff to upload and manage student results while enabling students to
securely access their academic reports. Despite its effectiveness, AVERS currently lacks

accessibility features tailored for students with disabilities, thereby limiting their independence in
accessing academic records.
Speech recognition technology that enables hands-free interactions with digital systems presents a
viable solution. For instance, AI-driven voice assistants have been successfully integrated into
assistive technologies for visually impaired individuals, improving their engagement with digital
platforms. This study focuses on designing and implementing a voice-based result system that
integrates seamlessly with AVERS, providing physically challenged students with the ability to
access their academic results independently.
The adoption of AVERS at Adekunle Ajasin University has significantly improved result processing
efficiency and privacy for students. However, physically challenged students, particularly those
with visual impairments, motor disabilities, or learning disorders such as dyslexia, face significant
barriers in independently accessing their academic records. This reliance on third-party assistance
compromises the privacy and autonomy that AVERS seeks to provide.
The absence of inclusive features in AVERS underscores the need for a voice-based solution that
allows physically challenged students to access their results independently. By integrating voice
automation, this research aims to enhance accessibility and inclusivity within the university’s
academic system. This research aims to enhance the existing AVERS system by integrating voice-
based automation, making it more accessible to physically challenged students. The primary
objectives include improving AVERS to accommodate their specific needs, incorporating voice
automation for all system operations to promote inclusiveness, and developing a robust noise-
filtering mechanism to enhance voice command accuracy.
The proposed system offers several key benefits. First, it enables physically challenged students to
access their academic results independently, eliminating the need for third-party assistance.
Additionally, the integration of voice automation will enhance overall usability, making AVERS
more user-friendly for all students. Ultimately, this advancement fosters a more inclusive academic
environment by addressing accessibility challenges and ensuring equal opportunities for all learners.
Unlike conventional result processing systems, this study integrates an adaptive ANN-based speech
recognition module tailored for academic environments, addressing both accessibility challenges
and real-world usability constraints.

2. Literature Review
2.1 Electronic Result Processing Systems
Electronic result processing systems have become essential in managing academic records
efficiently and securely. Amadin and Ukaoha [2] developed a computerized result system for the
Computer Science Department at the University of Benin, emphasizing the use of object-oriented
software design to streamline result management. Their findings highlighted significant
improvements in administrative efficiency and data security but revealed a lack of features catering
for physically challenged students. Bharamagoudar et al. [3] proposed a simple interface for
maintaining students' information, which emphasized data integrity and minimized human errors
associated with manual result processing. Similarly, Ekpenyong [4] developed an Intelligent
Knowledge-Based System (IKBS) for result processing using Microsoft Excel and programming
tools to improve data consistency. These systems lacked inclusivity features for physically
challenged students. The introduction of automated result systems has transformed educational
institutions by reducing the administrative burden and enhancing student privacy. However, these
systems often overlook the needs of physically challenged students, necessitating more inclusive
digital solutions.

2.2 Voice-Based Systems
Voice-based systems have gained prominence as an interface for hands-free interaction with digital
devices. Aditi et al. [5] highlighted the integration of voice recognition with home automation

2.3 Related Works
2.3.1 Academic Result Systems for Physically Challenged Students
Onyesolu and Ezenwegbu [8] proposed a computer-based testing system with voice commands
designed for examination environments in Nigeria. Their findings indicated that incorporating voice
commands into academic systems could improve accessibility for physically challenged students.
However, their research was limited to test-taking scenarios and did not extend to result
management systems. Ekpenyong [4] developed a result computation system for tertiary institutions
but highlighted its limitations in accommodating visually impaired and motor-disabled students.
They recommended incorporating assistive technologies, such as screen readers and voice
commands, to make academic systems more inclusive.

2.3.2 Advances in Speech Recognition for Educational Applications
Mahawar et al. [9] explored adaptive speech recognition systems that dynamically learn and adjust
to new voice commands. Their research demonstrated high accuracy in controlled environments but
highlighted challenges in real-world settings, particularly for individuals with speech impairments.
They recommended adopting neural networks for better adaptability.
Bourlard et al. [10] proposed a hybrid system combining Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) and
Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) for speech recognition tasks. Their study demonstrated superior
accuracy in noisy environments compared to traditional HMM models. This advancement laid the
groundwork for modern voice-based systems used in educational and accessibility technologies.
Due to the slow training and inference speed, this approach was not ideal for real-time speech
recognition, especially on the limited computing hardware available in 1990.

2.3.3 Voice-Based Automation in Educational Institutions
Anisha et al. [11] developed a voice and gesture-controlled smart vehicle integrated with home
automation. Although focused on mobility, their findings underscored the importance of combining
multiple input modalities to improve system usability for physically challenged users. They
recommended similar approaches for educational systems to enhance accessibility.
Aditi et al. [5] examined the use of IoT and Bluetooth-based technologies to control smart devices
through voice commands. Their study highlighted the potential for such systems to be extended to
educational environments, particularly in resource-constrained institutions.
The work of Johnson et al. [12] contributes to student support systems in Nigerian tertiary
institutions by designing an Interactive Voice Response System (IVRS) integrated with existing
student information systems. The system leverages Computer-Telephony Integration (CTI) and
mobile computing to provide students with timely access to academic information via their mobile
devices, reducing errors, stress, delays, and long queues associated with manual processes. Using
the Rapid Application Development approach, the system is implemented with VOXEO Prophecy
Server, HTML, Ajax-PHP, WAMP Server, and MySQL. It enhances efficiency in academic
administration by enabling real-time information access and flexible report generation for
management decision-making.

2.4 Appraisal of Literature
The literature review highlights several key insights. Despite advancements in voice-based
technologies, there is limited research on their application in academic result systems for physically
challenged students. Real-world implementations of voice recognition systems face challenges,
particularly in noisy environments, which reduces their effectiveness in educational settings [9], [7].
However, the emergence of AI-based models, such as the hybrid HMM-ANN approach
demonstrated by Bourlard et al. [10], has paved the way for more adaptable speech recognition
systems. Additionally, Onyesolu and Ezenwegbu [8] stress the need for inclusive design, advocating
for the integration of voice commands to enhance system accessibility.
To improve academic result systems for physically challenged students, several recommendations
are proposed. Integrating adaptive speech recognition powered by neural networks can enhance
accuracy by learning user-specific speech patterns. Developing intuitive, voice-responsive
interfaces will enable seamless navigation, ensuring accessibility for all users. Additionally,
incorporating advanced noise-filtering technologies will improve system performance in noisy
environments. Lastly, prioritizing scalability and inclusivity will ensure that the system
accommodates diverse needs, fostering a more inclusive academic experience.
Despite advancements in automated result systems, many of these solutions lack dedicated
accessibility features, particularly for students with physical impairments. Existing research [9], [7]
highlights challenges in real-world implementation, such as noise interference and limited
adaptability. This study addresses these gaps by employing ANN and HMM models for more robust
speech recognition in academic settings.

Furthermore, while advanced speech recognition models such as the hybrid HMM-ANN proposed

3. Methodology
3.1 Data Collection
This study employs the observation method to assess the accessibility challenges of physically
challenged students at Adekunle Ajasin University. The university accommodates around 20,000
students in the year 2022 with an average of 5% being physically challenged which is around 1,000
students. According to available estimates, a small but significant percentage of students in the
institution have disabilities that hinder their independent access to academic resources. The research
does not rely on direct surveys but instead derives insights from the usability challenges faced by
such students.
For model development, the Speech Commands Dataset from Kaggle was utilized, which contains
approximately 102,000 labeled audio samples across multiple speech commands suitable for system
navigation. While the dataset was pre-existing and not collected from Adekunle Ajasin University
students, custom samples were recorded by the researchers for testing and adaptation. These test
samples simulated real interaction scenarios within AVERS. Although physically challenged
participants were not directly involved in the model training phase, the system was designed with
their needs in mind based on observational insights, and future iterations will incorporate direct
usability testing involving this demographic.
The Speech Commands Dataset used for model training consists of approximately 102,000 labeled
audio samples across 35 distinct spoken command classes (e.g., “go,” “stop,” “yes,” “no,” “left,”
“right”). Each sample is a 1-second audio clip recorded at 16 kHz. These commands were selected
due to their relevance in controlling academic system navigation. To adapt the system for AVERS-
specific operations, the researcher also recorded additional custom voice samples (e.g., “check
result,” “logout”) using local accents and voice patterns. These supplementary samples were used
solely for testing and real-world simulation.
Audio data underwent preprocessing using Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) to extract
relevant spectral features. Background noise filtering was applied, and data augmentation was
performed using pitch shifting, time stretching, and Gaussian noise injection to simulate noisy
university environments. This helped in improving robustness against real-world conditions.
The final training dataset comprised 102,000 samples from Kaggle's Speech Commands Dataset
plus 500 custom-recorded samples (50 samples each for 10 AVERS-specific commands), totaling

3.2 Approach
This study adopts a hybrid approach combining Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) and Artificial
Neural Networks (ANNs) for speech recognition. HMMs model temporal dependencies in speech
sequences, while ANNs, particularly Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), handle complex pattern
classification and adapt to new pronunciations.
For training the voice automation system for AVERS, a publicly available dataset from Kaggle was
used. The Speech Commands Dataset on Kaggle, containing 102000 audio samples, was selected
due to its suitability for command-based voice automation. This dataset consists of different spoken
words, including commands like “go,” “stop,” “left,” and numbers which can be adapted for system
navigation and result retrieval.
The dataset was preprocessed using Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) to extract key
speech features while filtering out background noise. Data augmentation techniques, such as pitch
shifting, time stretching, and noise addition, were applied to improve model robustness as shown in
Figure 3.
The model was trained using a hybrid approach, combining Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) and

3.5 Combination of Hidden Markov Model (HMM) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) for
Speech Recognition
Recent advancements in speech recognition have explored the integration of Hidden Markov
Models (HMMs) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) to address the limitations inherent in using
these models independently. HMMs are effective for modeling sequential data but assume that
speech signals are piecewise stationary, which limits their adaptability. Conversely, ANNs,
particularly Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), excel in classifying patterns without assumptions
about statistical distributions.
Bourlard [10] demonstrated that combining HMMs and MLPs in a hybrid system can enhance
speech recognition accuracy by leveraging the strengths of both models. In this architecture, MLPs
compute the emission probabilities for each state of the HMM. The training process involves
minimizing a Mean Square Error (MSE) (1) criterion to optimize the MLP parameters for accurate
probability estimation.


                                                                                           (1)
This hybrid approach offers significant advantages as it supports discriminative training which
utilizes a posteriori probability approximation, which inherently improves classification accuracy.
Also, contextual adaptation enables feeding MLPs with larger feature windows allows the system
to capture time correlations between successive acoustic vectors, enhancing recognition in complex
environments. The training process involves iterative optimization using a Viterbi algorithm to label
feature sequences, followed by backpropagation to update MLP weights and HMM transition
probabilities. This iterative method continues until optimal performance is achieved.

5. Results and Discussion
5.1 Findings
The system was evaluated using three key metrics: accuracy, response time, and user satisfaction.
It demonstrated a commendable accuracy rate of 92% in recognizing voice commands, ensuring
reliable interactions. The response time remained within acceptable limits, averaging just 2 seconds,
allowing for a seamless user experience. Additionally, feedback from test users indicated high levels
of satisfaction, primarily due to the system’s enhanced accessibility and ease of use.
While direct participation of physically challenged students was not involved in this prototype
phase, the system’s test environment simulated their common usage scenarios using pre-defined
interaction flows. Ethical considerations were maintained, and the system is scheduled for usability
testing with real users in future deployment phases. Structured feedback will be gathered using
System Usability Scale (SUS) surveys and informal interviews, subject to ethical approval by the
university’s research board.
Baseline Accuracy Comparison: The voice-based results system achieved an accuracy of 92%.
For comparison, Google Voice, a leading speech recognition system, typically achieves an accuracy
of around 95% in controlled environments. However, Google Voice is a general-purpose solution,
whereas this model is optimized for the academic context, specifically result retrieval for students.

only system performed slightly better at 87%, confirming that each model had strengths and
limitations when used in isolation. The hybrid HMM-ANN system outperformed both, achieving
92% accuracy, which reflects the improved sequential pattern recognition of HMMs combined with
the non-linear classification power of ANNs. This validates the decision to integrate both techniques
for optimal recognition in educational use cases.
In interpreting these results further, the hybrid system demonstrated greater robustness to real-world
disruptions, especially background noise typical in campus environments such as lecture halls or
cybercafés. During testing with artificially introduced ambient noise (classroom chatter, fans, low-
volume music), the system retained over 88% accuracy, compared to ANN-only which dropped to
78%, and HMM-only which fell to 72%. Additionally, the system was tested using voice samples
with Yoruba-influenced English accents, revealing that it maintained over 90% recognition rate with
minimal confusion in localized pronunciations (e.g., “check” sounding like “chek”).
Although the system was not explicitly trained on impaired speech, tests simulating mild speech
disorders (slurred or slow pronunciation) showed the model retained approximately 85% accuracy,
highlighting its adaptability and potential with further fine-tuning or personalization. This affirms
the system’s capability to support physically challenged students, including those with mild speech
difficulties, ensuring accessible interaction without third-party assistance.

5.2 Hardware and Deployment
Deployment Model: The solution is currently deployed on-premises, installed locally on a browser.
However, it has the potential to be cloud-based and directly integrated into AVERS for remote
accessibility.
Hardware Requirements: For optimal performance, the system requires the following
configuration:
     a. Processor: Intel Core i5 (or equivalent) and above
     b. RAM: Minimum 8GB (Recommended 16GB for large-scale operations)
     c. Storage: 256GB SSD (Recommended: 512GB SSD for faster processing)
     d. Operating System: Windows 10/11, macOS, or Linux (Ubuntu preferred)
     e. Software Dependencies: Python 3.8+, TensorFlow, PyTorch, and necessary speech
        recognition libraries
Accessibility Considerations: The system can be accessed using either a mobile phone or a PC.
While an external microphone is not required, the use of a hands-free earpiece with a microphone
is recommended for better speech recognition in noisy environments.

5.3 Current Limitations and Future User Studies
This prototype phase did not involve direct testing with physically challenged students, representing
a significant limitation. Future work will include:
    a. IRB-approved user studies with 30-50 physically challenged students
    b. Structured usability evaluation using SUS questionnaires
    c. Task completion rate analysis for core AVERS functions
    d. Accessibility compliance assessment following WCAG 2.1 guidelines
    e. Real-world deployment testing in university computer labs

6. Conclusion and Future Work
This study successfully developed a voice-based electronic result system for Adekunle Ajasin
University’s AVERS, enhancing accessibility for physically challenged students. The
implementation of ANN-based speech recognition with the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) proved
effective in providing a hands-free, inclusive solution.
The system achieved a recognition accuracy of 92%, an F1-score of 0.91, and an average response
time of 2 seconds. These outcomes demonstrate high usability, responsiveness, and adaptability for
real-world academic environments.
However, the current system version has some limitations. It lacks real-time personalization for
users with severe speech impairments and does not yet support indigenous languages or voice input
in noisy, uncontrolled environments beyond those simulated in testing. Additionally, it has not been
fully deployed for live student use, and structured usability feedback is pending.
Future research should explore multi-language support, prioritizing indigenous languages with tonal
variations. Additionally, pilot deployment at Adekunle Ajasin University will provide real-world
feedback to refine the system further. Plans are underway to incorporate speaker-adaptive models,
integrate advanced noise-cancellation algorithms, and conduct usability studies with physically
challenged users under ethical review.
By incorporating these enhancements, the solution can further strengthen inclusive education and
ensure that students with physical disabilities can access academic results independently.
