Gathering Requirements for Stress Management in Autistic
Adults for the Development of Augmented Reality
Self-Interventions
JOHN J. MCGOWAN and IAIN P. MCGREGOR, School of Computing, Engineering, and the Built
Environment, Edinburgh Napier University, Edinburgh, United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern
Ireland

Autistic adults typically encounter multiple daily stressors, particularly in workplace settings or public spaces,
often resulting in sensory overload. To address these challenges, technological interventions have previously
been developed to alleviate stress, enhance cognitive functioning and overall well-being. Tools such as mobile
devices, virtual reality (VR), and augmented reality (AR) applications have shown promise in supporting and
advancing clinical practices by utilizing sensory-based technologies. In this article, an extension of a published
2023 survey, we present the second phase of research concerning a series of semi-structured interviews
with autistic participants and caregivers. We report on the identification of currently used technological
preferences, and the perceived potential for new technologies. Coping mechanisms employed and reactions to
stress are detailed, trigger locations and stressful scenarios are identified, as well as masking techniques used
by autistic adults. The results from the study have identified interest in developing new applications based on
existing technologies, with aural sensitivity identified as the main stimuli that needs managed. Participants
highlighted the importance of planning as a coping mechanism and indicated that familiarity, in audio and
visual feedback, helps to calm participants in times of stress. Respondents commented on existing forms of
audio-visual distractors used by autistic people to cope with stressful scenarios, for example, the use of music,
and stimming, which could be employed within potential applications to reduce stress. The overarching goal of
this research is to address the question, “How can the potentially debilitating effects of stress in autistic adults
be mitigated by a self-customizable approach to the design of accessible technologies?” The insights gained
will inform future research regarding the design of technological self-interventions for stress management in
autistic adults. The findings further identify practical design requirements such as adaptive audio masking,
context-aware planning tools, and customizable multimodal feedback to support real-time stress regulation.

1   Introduction
This research is concerned with the identification and management of stress among autistic people,
along with the use and adoption of technologies those on the autism spectrum use in helping
address stress. Qualitative data from a series of semi-structured interviews were used to enhance
the understanding from the results of an initial survey [91], feeding into baseline requirements
for the design and development of a proposed stress management augmented reality (AR) tool.
Technological and non-technological self-interventions have been designed to help autistic people
overcome stress-related challenges, for example, in communication, social interaction and in living
independently [14, 23]. Previous studies indicated that technology demonstrated major benefits
and interest for autistic people [18, 105]. Ideally, however, software designers need to raise their
awareness about the variety of issues autistic people face. For example, their cultural background
and level of access to technology, to help understand that developing future technology-based
products requires more than mere effectiveness [74]. A 2023 review of assistive technologies for
autistic adults identified a general lack of requirements-driven design involving the target audience,
compounded by non-standardized evaluation processes [152]. The authors commented that few
studies interviewed target users themselves, often due to the difficulties associated with autistic
interviewees feeling comfortable in communicating their needs. Consequently, it is key to prioritize
meeting important user goals while aligning with individual users’ distinct interests and abilities.
   The growth of assistive technologies for autistic individuals has seen developments in both hard-
ware and software. Wearable devices with biosensors enable real-time monitoring of physiological
indicators, for example, heartrate and eye gaze, aiming to facilitate personalized interventions
to manage stress [72]. Virtual reality (VR) and AR platforms offer digital environments and
augmentation for therapeutic interventions, such as exposure therapy [88] and social skills training
[38], providing a more controlled setting for autistic individuals to cope with sensitivity issues
and generalize social skills. More recently, advancements in AI have led to the development of
intelligent systems capable of analyzing behavioral patterns and providing tailored feedback and
support in real-time [63]. Examples include AI-driven communication aids that adapt to the user’s
preferences and needs, enhancing their ability to express themselves effectively. As technology
continues to evolve, the availability of assistive technology for autistic individuals should ideally
be offering increasingly sophisticated customized solutions, to address their diverse needs, and
promote independence and inclusion, based on personal data and use. However, the underlying
complexities of the technology used should be hidden from the users, as they will not require a
comparable learning curve to interact with the system.
   This article is an expanded version of a conference paper [91] presented at ASSETS’ 23. That
paper focused on results from an initial study where 202 participants, including autistic adults and
caregivers of autistic people, completed an online survey. The data collected related to common
triggers and sensory information recognized within autistic people, as well as from caregivers
who may identify catalysts. Additionally, reflections on contemporary assistive technologies were
provided, along with where potential may lie in using alternative applications, as a means of
assisting high-functioning autistic adults. The article discussed the potential of technology-based
self-interventions to mitigate stress among autistic adults. Examples of such interventions included
wearable devices, like smartwatches, for real-time monitoring of biometric information, mobile
applications offering personalized coping strategies and extended reality (XR) environments for
immersive relaxation exercises. By designing technological solutions tailored to the specific needs
and preferences of autistic adults, the study demonstrated the potential to empower individuals to
manage stress more effectively and enhance their overall well-being. The current article underlines
the importance of understanding stress triggers in autistic adults and highlights the promising role

2     Background and Related Work
This section discusses the background and challenges associated with autism spectrum disorder
(ASD) and stress, as well as some of the related work detailing multimodal interventions employed
to counter these challenges. In addition, effective methods of evaluating these interventions are
discussed.

2.1    Autism and Stress
ASD is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by a broad range of challenges in
social interaction, communication, and behavior [49]. It is aptly referred to as a spectrum condition
due to the variety of reactions that can vary widely across individuals. ASD encompasses a diverse
group of conditions, and its prevalence has been steadily increasing, affecting approximately 1
in 36 children in the United States according to the Center for Disease Control and Prevention
[84]. Many autistic people advocate for identity-first terminology concerning references to the
condition [9]. Subsequently, for this article, terms related to autistic participants will be employed,
for example, referring to autistic people or autistic participants.
   Autistic people may be challenged with understanding and using verbal and non-verbal com-
munication, interpreting social cues, and engaging in reciprocal relationships [81, 99, 109]. This
can lead to challenges in forming and maintaining friendships, as well as understanding body
language and the unspoken rules of social interaction. Camouflaging is a common mechanism
for coping, which can often result in negative mental health consequences, as can attempting to
“pass as normal” or adaptive morphing [24, 34]. Sensory sensitivities are common among autistic
individuals, who may experience heightened or diminished responses to sensory stimuli such as
unpredictable sounds, certain types of lighting, undesired textures, and unwanted smells [124].
Sensory challenges can impact daily functioning, making certain environments overwhelming or
uncomfortable. For example, in the workplace, human and mechanical sounds, as well as strip
lighting, can be considered stressors, or on public transport, where olfactory, temperature, and
touch stimulus can cause additional problems [151]. Subsequently, this can lead to high levels of
stress in different scenarios, as over-stimulation can negatively affect cognitive functioning [83].
   Repetitive behaviors and a preference for routine can be characteristic for autistic people as
a coping mechanism, providing a sense of predictability and agency [138]. Autistic individuals
may also engage in self-stimulating behavior (stimming), have intense interests in specific topics,
and resist change in their daily routines as alternative ways to manage sensory overload [12].
One prominent challenge autistic individuals face is the co-occurrence of comorbid conditions,
including intellectual disabilities, epilepsy, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD),
anxiety, and depression [29]. Addressing these are important for providing comprehensive care and
support. In dealing with sensory challenges, autistic adults may use masking techniques within
social situations, as a means of disguising their condition, which can cause stress and lead to further
health issues [61].
   Access to appropriate educational and employment opportunities can be taxing for autistic people,
as well as the working environments that neurotypical people do not find problematic [151]. For
example, open-plan offices with fluorescent lighting can present challenges to neurodiverse workers,
while not affecting neurotypical employees, as unwanted sound and light can be overwhelming
stressors [55]. Subsequently, tailoring educational strategies to meet autistic individuals’ unique
learning styles and providing workplace accommodations can significantly enhance success in
academic and professional settings [161]. A recent study by Ara et al. [8] highlighted some of

2.2    Related Work—Current Interventions
Current interventions for alleviating stress in autistic individuals include both technological and
non-technological approaches. By integrating these approaches, comprehensive support systems
can be tailored to individual needs, beyond promoting well-being and adaptive functioning in
autistic individuals.

   2.2.1 Non-Technological. Non-technological strategies include cognitive-behavioral therapy
(CBT), which has shown to be effective in treating acute stress and supported by empirical evidence
[28]; however, other studies have also demonstrated a variety of efficacy for autistic people and
that more evidence is required [153]. Other cognitive therapies include mindfulness practices,
and sensory integration therapy, which aim to modify thought patterns, promote relaxation, and
improve sensory processing [1]. Meditation has also been seen to be a useful activity to reduce stress
for autistic people, in a bid to overcome sensory overload and focus on directing and determining
which senses to focus on [130]. In addition, the use of music, both listening to and participation, is
well-established as an intervention that has shown significant potential in improving the oral-motor
activities of autistic people, as well as having a positive impact upon social communication, motor
skills, and reducing stress levels [21, 58]. Studies that use yoga and mindfulness-based programs as
supplemental interventions, for autistic children and adolescents, suggest efficacy in a number of
areas. Although few studies have reported improvements in the core symptoms of autism, they
may improve a variety of prosocial behaviors, including communication and imitative behaviors;
increased tolerance of sitting and of adult proximity; self-control; and social communication [131].
For example, Radhakrishna [117] employed and Integrated Approach to Yoga Therapy where the
authors reported improvements in communication, imitative behaviors, and motor control. Singh
et al. [134] conducted studies using a mindfulness technique known as “Meditation on the Soles of
the Feet” to help manage aggressive impulses of youth with Asperger Syndrome. Findings indicated
that all three adolescents reduced instances of their aggressive behaviors from a mean of 14–20
per week during baseline, to 4–6 per week during mindfulness training, subsequently to zero rates
during the last 4 weeks of intervention. Additionally, reductions in lethargy, social withdrawal, and
non-compliance, were observed [22]. However, the need for additional evidence-based research is
key in supporting these findings.

of a year (for example, interacting with media, engaging in exercise, playing games, attending
events, and participating in other social activities) and found a significant increase in the four
factors of quality of life that were measured. These included satisfaction, independence, competence,
and social interaction, as well as the total score for quality of life from baseline to the end of the
intervention 12 months later. Leisure activities that do not require high skill levels and promote
team environments have been recommended for autistic individuals, for example, rock climbing. In

   Another form of common non-technological (and technological—see Section 2.2.2) intervention

cubes or fidget spinners; squishy and tactile play toys create a tactile sensory feedback mechanism
designed to reduce stress [146].

   2.2.2 Technological. In addressing sensitivity and stress in autism, technology can play an
important role. One common approach involves using headphones to listen to music as a positive
distractor [133] and to employ noise cancelation features to address auditory sensitivity [62].
Headphones help individuals regulate auditory stimuli by listening to their preferred music or white
noise while reducing external noises as an aid to managing external auditory stimuli. Sunglasses
are also used to mitigate sensitivity to different forms of lighting, as they can filter and reduce the
intensity of visual stimuli, enhancing comfort and reducing distress [100].
   Stim toys are items or props sought by the participants or provided by carers. Less formal
than a therapeutic intervention, they are designed for autistic children and adults for sensory
regulation [48]. They offer tactile stimulation for self-soothing and redirecting attention from
overwhelming sensory inputs [31]. Mobile technologies provide access to specialized apps designed
for individuals with autism [139]. These apps offer a variety of functions, including sensory regula-
tion exercises, visual schedules, and communication aids [100]. Mobile technologies aim to allow
autistic individuals to manage their sensory experiences and navigate social or working contexts
independently [149].
   Wearable technologies, equipped with biosensors and AI algorithms, can monitor physiological
indicators of stress in real-time [75]. They can provide personalized biometric feedback, for example,
heartrate and related individual arousal levels. Some applications offer relaxation techniques or
sensory grounding exercises through mobile technologies, including XR platforms [29]. Developing
XR technologies demonstrate potential for creating immersive environments for desensitization
therapy and social skills training, for example, providing relaxation experiences, such as virtual
nature scenes or calming activities which promote relaxation and emotional regulation [26, 143].
However, care must be taken with XR use not to isolate target users from the real world as concerns
exist regarding the introduction of tools that may encourage obsessive behaviors or social rigidity, in
particular, via VR headsets which may further contribute to autistic individuals’ avoidance of social
situations and lead to isolation from their peers [89, 101, 141]. By integrating diverse technological
modalities, support systems can be established to promote the well-being and adaptive functioning
of individuals with autism in stressful situations.

5     Discussion
The results reiterate and further develop many of the themes derived from the first study [91].
This included how familiarity of people, locations, and routine aids in reinforcement of calming
capabilities and reduction of stress in autistic individuals. Unpredictability, most commonly of
sound, and the actions of people, causes stress and requires a more robust approach regarding
the development of solutions. The use of positive distractors, whether audio, visual, or tactile, is
currently used by participants in their own ways; however, there is much scope for development
of technological solutions that could be used in ad hoc locations and scenarios as a form of self-
intervention.

manage anxiety; real-time biometric monitoring to avoid overwhelming stimuli; the use of sensory

devices. These guidelines reflect some of the findings in the present research; however, the authors
noted that the study was small in scale and required implementation and testing for validation
of its proposals. Hedges et al. [57] surveyed 472 autistic adolescents regarding technology use
in managing stress. They reported widespread use of mobile technologies to support work, act
as reminders and planning tools, or as ways to relax at home via audio, gaming, and video. The
authors proposed that self-intervention technologies could be used to reduce anxiety and stress;
however, they suggested there was a need to minimize potential obsessive use of devices. Contrary
to this idea, other research indicated some obsessive behaviors displayed by autistic people tend to
be related to preventing or reducing the discomfort that obsessive thought produces, suggesting
this was a positive means of calming themselves [108]. Hoeberichts et al. [59] developed a mobile

generates an overview of the patients’ stress, and suggests ways to cope, for example, “go for a
walk,” “do a breathing exercise” or “listen to some music.” The study generated positive results;
however, the authors contend that the results may have been influenced by the participants who
helped to design the app. In addition, the app does not present real-time solutions but is based
on post-analysis of voluntary participant information. Kalantari et al. [65] looked at wearable

reactions in a similar way to the present research, as well support types which included redirection,

AR frameworks for adults.

5.1   General Requirements
Data from this research suggest that the use of existing technology for new applications is favored
over purchasing new devices, a theme reflected in the SATORI study [46]. Economic reasons were
highlighted by autistic participants to support this idea, which have been further exacerbated
in the past few years through rising living costs, COVID, and a subsequent rise in demand for

5.4   Biometric Monitoring
Participants commented on issues regarding sensory overload through hypersensitivity to external
stimuli. Additionally, issues were identified regarding hypo sensitivity, which has led to issues
with physical ill health, and long-term mental health problems. This relationship which has been
previously identified in autism research where negative emotionality, depression, and anxiety
symptoms were identified in autistic children and adolescents [17, 112]. Subsequently, it is proposed
that an app could monitor physical biometrics in real-time, for example, heartrate, eye movement,
stimming, user-defined gestures, hydration levels, or changes in voice. The users would be alerted
through audio or haptic feedback as an indication that attention is required. Security issues of any
potential AR application need to be considered, especially when dealing with personal information.
A recent review highlighted some of the issues that exist with current cyber security protocols which
rely on traditional cryptographic protocols such as Transport Layer Security and Virtual Private
Networks [13]. None of these secure communication solutions ensures the preservation of the
integrity of the captured data end-to-end, as such protocols are known to have Man-in-the-Middle
vulnerabilities. The authors suggested that further research and development of security-based
tools for AR should be considered. Subsequently, development of new AR-based prototypes should
ensure that data is kept within the system until such times as a more robust solution for securing
data exists.

6 Conclusions
The current work presents a novel dataset of qualitative data from five caregivers and 15 autistic
individuals. The dataset extends the findings from an initial survey highlighting some common
themes, including familiarity, unpredictability, the use of positive distractors as coping mechanisms,
and an interest in AR technologies. Auditory sensitivity remains the primary source of stress-related
reactions.
   Regarding the overarching goal of mitigating the potentially debilitating effects of stress, pro-
posals have been made regarding future work and the potential for prototype developments for
use on users’ existing mobile devices and headphones, allowing the use of audio noise canceling or
listen-through capabilities when required. Monitoring of real-time biometric data that can measure
and pre-empt the impact of stress is proposed by presenting the user with appropriate distractors,
or by providing digital assistance dependent on the given situation, based upon learned behaviors
via an AI reinforcement learning method. User feedback should be customized using audio, visual,
and haptic feedback. Audio could be used to mask unwanted sounds within stressful locations
or augment existing soundscapes with more pleasing sounds determined by the user. The use of
familiar voices could also provide a calming distraction. Similarly, visual stimuli could be presented
