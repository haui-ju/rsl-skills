## Abstract
<a id="abstract"></a>

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is rapidly changing higher education, offering new op- portunities to support diverse learners, including students with dyslexia, while rais- ing important questions about ethics and assessment. Existing work on Universal Design for Learning (UDL) and assistive technologies highlights persistent barriers in reading, writing and planning, but there is limited guidance on how generative AI (GenAI) can be integrated into academic work safely and accessibly. This study addresses that gap through a convergent parallel mixed-methods design with quali- tative priority (QUAL + quan). Semi-structured interviews with specialist Student Support and Wellbeing Services (SSWS) were conducted alongside an online stu- dent survey to identify recurring challenges, current tool use and attitudes towards AI. The analysis reveals difficulties with academic writing, critical reading and time management, patchy and sometimes unsustainable adoption of assistive tools and AI, and uncertainty about acceptable AI use and its implications for academic in- tegrity. In response, the paper proposes a dual-component, UDL-aligned system for responsible AI-supported assessment. The instructor-facing AI Engagement Spec- trum helps academics set clear, assignment-specific rules for permissible AI use, grounded in constructive alignment. The student-facing AI Scaffold translates these rules into a structured workflow that guides students with dyslexia through reading, planning, drafting and revising with AI support while preserving authorship and critical engagement. The primary contribution is a transferable framework that links institutional policy, pedagogical intent and students’ study practices. It offers SS- WSs, lecturers and educational designers a practical starting point for implementing AI in ways that are accessible, transparent and focused on supporting students with dyslexia.

Abstract
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is rapidly changing higher education, offering new op-
portunities to support diverse learners, including students with dyslexia, while rais-
ing important questions about ethics and assessment. Existing work on Universal
Design for Learning (UDL) and assistive technologies highlights persistent barriers
in reading, writing and planning, but there is limited guidance on how generative
AI (GenAI) can be integrated into academic work safely and accessibly. This study
addresses that gap through a convergent parallel mixed-methods design with quali-
tative priority (QUAL + quan). Semi-structured interviews with specialist Student
Support and Wellbeing Services (SSWS) were conducted alongside an online stu-
dent survey to identify recurring challenges, current tool use and attitudes towards
AI. The analysis reveals difficulties with academic writing, critical reading and time
management, patchy and sometimes unsustainable adoption of assistive tools and
AI, and uncertainty about acceptable AI use and its implications for academic in-
tegrity. In response, the paper proposes a dual-component, UDL-aligned system for
responsible AI-supported assessment. The instructor-facing AI Engagement Spec-
trum helps academics set clear, assignment-specific rules for permissible AI use,
grounded in constructive alignment. The student-facing AI Scaffold translates these
rules into a structured workflow that guides students with dyslexia through reading,
planning, drafting and revising with AI support while preserving authorship and
critical engagement. The primary contribution is a transferable framework that links
institutional policy, pedagogical intent and students’ study practices. It offers SS-
WSs, lecturers and educational designers a practical starting point for implementing
AI in ways that are accessible, transparent and focused on supporting students with
dyslexia.

1 Introduction

Dyslexia is a common specific learning difference, estimated to affect around 5–10%
of the population worldwide (Snowling, 2019), with significant implications for uni-
versity-level study. Students with dyslexia often experience persistent difficulties in
reading, spelling and written expression, alongside working-memory and process-
ing-load constraints that affect planning and organisation (NHS, 2022; Richardson
& Wydell, 2003; Swanson, 2020a). In practice, this can make it harder to keep up
with reading lists, extract key points from dense texts and produce extended written
assignments, contributing to attainment gaps and higher attrition risks compared to
their peers (Richardson & Wydell, 2003). These challenges are amplified in digital
learning environments, where most materials are delivered via screens and students
are expected to juggle multiple online sources and platforms.
    Universities commonly respond through a mix of individual accommodations
and broader commitments to Universal Design for Learning (UDL). UDL advocates
designing curricula that offer multiple means of engagement, representation and
action/expression from the outset so that variability in learners’ profiles, including
dyslexia, is anticipated rather than treated as an exception (CAST, 2024; Rose &
Meyer, 2002). For students with dyslexia, this can mean routine access to alternative
representations (for example, Text-to-Speech (TTS), visual organisers and chunked
instructions) and flexible ways of demonstrating understanding (Bäck et al., 2024;
Rao, 2020). However, putting UDL into practice consistently remains challenging
(Fovet, 2020; Rao, 2020), and there is limited guidance on how to translate UDL prin-
ciples into day-to-day study workflows and assessment practices for specific groups
such as students with dyslexia. Recent work has begun to explore UDL-informed
approaches to AI curricula and inclusive AI education more broadly (Saborío-Tay-
lor & Rojas-Ramírez, 2024; Song et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025b), but a concrete,
operational framework for combining UDL with AI support in university contexts is
still missing. The rapid emergence of AI-powered study tools extends this assistive
technology landscape, but institutions currently offer limited concrete, UDL-aligned
guidance on how such tools should be integrated into everyday study workflows for
students with dyslexia.
    Alongside traditional assistive technologies such as TTS, dictation, grammar
checkers and mind-mapping tools (Lerga et al., 2021; Paudel & Acharya, 2024;
Zallio & Ohashi, 2022), the rapid emergence of Generative Artificial Intelligence
(GenAI) systems has created new possibilities for supporting students with dyslexia.
Tools such as ChatGPT can summarise readings, suggest outlines, propose alterna-
tive phrasings and offer near-instant feedback on drafts (Holmes et al., 2019; Ji et
al., 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023). Used carefully, these capabilities can reduce the time
and effort spent on decoding dense text or overcoming “blank page” paralysis, allow-
ing students to focus more on comprehension, idea generation and argumentation

2 Literature review

This review synthesises research on dyslexia, assistive technologies, GenAI and
assessment to motivate a UDL-aligned AI workflow for students with dyslexia. It
outlines key academic challenges and existing supports, examines GenAI’s affor-
dances and risks (including cognitive offloading and integrity concerns), and posi-
tions constructive alignment and UDL as the pedagogical lenses underpinning the AI
Engagement Spectrum and AI Scaffold framework.

2.1 Dyslexia in higher education

Students with dyslexia typically encounter pronounced difficulties with text-heavy
materials. Reading scholarly articles or lengthy textbooks can be slow and exhaust-
ing, often compromising comprehension, while extended writing may involve
problems with planning, organisation, and coherence (Mortimore & Crozier, 2006;
Snowling & Hulme, 2012). These challenges are associated with attainment gaps
and higher attrition rates in higher education (Pino & Mortari, 2014; Richardson &
Wydell, 2003).
    Difficulties can intensify in digital learning environments where most content is
screen-based. Eye-movement and screen-reading studies report more and longer fixa-
tions and greater cognitive effort in dyslexic readers, highlighting the need for acces-
sible presentation (Prado et al., 2007; Rello & Baeza-Yates, 2017; Le Cunff et al.,
2024). Managing multiple online sources also taxes attention and working memory,
which are often constrained in dyslexic learners (Swanson, 2020b; Le Cunff et al.,
2024). Support such as read-aloud technology can improve comprehension under
high load, reinforcing the case for accessible, multimodal materials (Schiavo et al.,
2021).
    Alongside these challenges, some studies suggest specific visual–spatial advan-
tages on global processing tasks, while other work finds no general superiority, indi-
cating any strengths are likely task-specific (von Károlyi et al., 2003; Winner et al.,
2001). Effective support should therefore leverage potential idea-generation/holistic
thinking strengths while mitigating barriers in text-intensive tasks (MacCullagh et
al., 2017; Mortimore & Crozier, 2006; Snowling & Hulme, 2012).

2.2 Existing assistive tools and their limitations

Universities commonly provide text-to-speech, speech-to-text, word prediction,
concept-mapping tools, and screen readers; students report better access, reduced
fatigue, and smoother drafting when these are part of routine workflows (Bäck et
al., 2024; Pino & Mortari, 2014). Concept mapping and related visual organisers can
aid organisation and understanding across domains, supporting planning demands
common in extended writing (Nesbit & Adesope, 2006). Beyond basic utilities, mul-
timodal supports that combine visual and auditory channels can improve comprehen-
sion under high load, especially when embedded within tasks rather than bolted on
Mayer (2009); Schiavo et al. (2021).

2.3 GenAI as a new support modality

The emergence of GenAI tools has introduced new ways to support learners, includ-
ing those with dyslexia. Large language models can summarise texts, generate expla-
nations, suggest structures, and provide examples in accessible language (Kasneci et
al., 2023; Weng et al., 2024). For students who struggle with decoding dense prose,
GenAI can act as a bridge: it can rephrase material, generate outlines, and offer step-
by-step prompts that sustain engagement with challenging tasks (Snowling & Hulme,
2012; Swanson, 2020b; Zhao et al., 2025a). GenAI can also provide rapid feedback
on draft text, enabling iterative refinement and experimentation with expression
(Chan & Hu, 2023; Weng et al., 2024).
    At the same time, GenAI differs from traditional assistive tools. Rather than
compensating only for decoding or transcription bottlenecks, it can generate new
content (arguments, examples, extended prose), which blurs the boundary between
support and substitution (Kirschner et al., 2006). Without clear guidance, students
may bypass core cognitive work; moreover, LLMs can produce fluent but incorrect
or fabricated content, requiring verification to avoid error propagation (Buçinca et al.,
2021; Ji et al., 2023).

2.4 The challenge of cognitive offloading in AI-supported learning

Cognitive offloading refers to the use of external tools or resources to reduce bur-
dens on internal cognitive processes, such as working memory or attention (Clark &
Chalmers, 1998; Risko & Gilbert, 2016). In education, well-designed offloading can
free up resources for higher-order thinking; for example, diagrams or checklists can
support planning so learners can focus on argumentation rather than logistics (Larkin
& Simon, 1987; Mayer, 2009). However, offloading becomes problematic when it
displaces essential cognitive work and thereby undermines learning (Kirschner et al.,
2006; Risko & Gilbert, 2016).
   GenAI intensifies these tensions. Tools that summarise readings, generate essay

2.5 Student attitudes toward assistive technology and AI

Students’ attitudes toward assistive and AI tools are often ambivalent: many value
tangible benefits (access, reduced effort, smoother drafting) yet simultaneously report
stigma or fear being perceived as “cheating” (McNicholl et al., 2021; Pino & Mortari,
2014). Among dyslexic students in particular, qualitative studies note that day-to-day
experiences with support, training, and the visibility of tool use shape confidence and
willingness to engage (Bäck et al., 2024; MacCullagh et al., 2017).
   Similar ambivalence appears around GenAI: students describe usefulness for
brainstorming, summarising, and feedback, but also worry about fairness, surveil-
lance, and misconduct boundaries (Chan & Hu, 2023; Weng et al., 2024). Those who
feel less confident in academic writing may be especially drawn to paraphrasing or
drafting assistance while remaining uncertain about what counts as legitimate sup-
port (Chan & Hu, 2023).
   Attitudes improve when expectations are explicit and support is integrated into
mainstream provision rather than bolted on: clear, ILO-aligned guidance normalises
appropriate use and reduces anxiety, whereas fragmented provision reinforces other-
ness (Pino & Mortari, 2014; Seale, 2013). Constructive alignment at the course level
helps delineate where tools may scaffold access or expression and where students
must demonstrate unaided cognitive work (Biggs, 1996).

2.7 Constructive alignment as framework for AI pedagogy

Constructive alignment turns high-level AI policies into concrete assessment design
by ensuring coherence between intended learning outcomes (ILOs), teaching–learn-

designed to elicit the cognitive evidence specified in the ILOs, rather than merely
grading products.

and creating (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001; Bloom et al., 1956). In the GenAI era,
this framing invites instructors to specify which aspects of task performance may
be legitimately supported by tools and which must be demonstrated unaided by the
student (Cotton et al., 2023; Weng et al., 2024).

exemplars, (b) asking students to critique or improve AI outputs, and (c) requiring
documentation and justification of AI use as part of the submission (Baig & Yade-
garidehkordi, 2024; Cotton et al., 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023). Studies also show
that transparency about expectations and AI’s role improves perceived fairness and
learning value (Chan & Hu, 2023; Luo, 2024). In short, applying constructive align-
ment here means treating AI use as part of the designed assessment process, not an
afterthought or merely a regulated risk (Biggs et al., 2023).

tasks, they can help maintain access while preserving academic standards (Rao et al.,

3 Methodology

This study used a convergent parallel mixed-methods design with qualitative priority
(QUAL + quan) to develop and refine an AI integration framework for supporting
students with dyslexia (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2017; Johnson & Onwuegbuzie,
2004). In a convergent parallel mixed methods design, qualitative and quantitative
data are collected during the same study phase, analysed separately, and then merged
for interpretation, and the notation QUAL + quan indicates qualitative priority in the
integration. Semi-structured interviews with SSWS formed the lead strand, providing
depth on challenges, existing practices and requirements from an expert perspective.
Alongside this, a cross-sectional student survey provided descriptive breadth on stu-
dents’ experiences and attitudes to AI tools in academic work. Figure 1 summarises
the mixed methods design, showing concurrent timing (QUAL + quan), separate
analyses, the integration step, and the two subsequent validation activities. The two
datasets were analysed separately and then integrated through comparison of themes
and survey patterns to inform the design of a structured AI integration pedagogical
system, followed by two layers of validation, review by SSWS and review against
established pedagogical principles. Together, these strands addressed our research
questions by characterising support needs and current tool use (RQ1–RQ2) and
informing the co-design and refinement of the AI Engagement Spectrum and AI Scaf-
fold framework (RQ3).

differences.

4 Findings from interviews and student survey

This section reports the key qualitative and quantitative findings from the SSWS
interviews and student survey that informed the design of the AI Scaffold framework
and AI Engagement Spectrum. The results are organised thematically: Section 4.1
focuses on the academic challenges faced by students with dyslexia in academic tasks
(RQ1); Section 4.2 examines current use of and adoption barriers around assistive
tools and AI-powered technologies (RQ1); and Section 4.3 explores students’ and
staff’s attitudes towards GenAI, including perceived benefits and concerns (RQ2).
A final subsection (Section 4.4) integrates these strands to show how the combined
evidence shaped the framework (RQ3).

4.1 Challenges faced by students with dyslexia in academic tasks

The interviews with SSWS reaffirmed many challenges documented in the dyslexia
literature while adding detail about how they play out in day-to-day university work.
Advisers described students who often have strong ideas and oral contributions but
struggle to convey these effectively in writing, especially in longer, formally assessed
assignments. Reading demands were reported as a constant pressure point: students
frequently fall behind on text-heavy modules and find it difficult to identify what is
essential in dense readings, which in turn affects their confidence in seminars and
exams. These challenges are compounded by organisational difficulties, such as
breaking large assignments into manageable steps and sustaining attention across
multiple online platforms. Table 1 summarises the main themes that emerged in rela-

and the use of assistive tools.

SSWS with well-developed ideas and insights but lack confidence in managing the
reading and writing demands of their programmes. Advisers noted that students often
avoid critical-analytical tasks or leave them to the last minute because they find it
hard to translate broad insights into structured arguments under time pressure. Plan-
ning and organisation difficulties mean that assignments can remain at the level of
notes or partial drafts for too long, contributing to elevated stress and lower attain-
ment. Staff also highlighted that students’ use of assistive tools is frequently reactive
and fragmented rather than embedded in coherent study routines. These observations
directly informed the design priorities for the AI Scaffold, which aims to support
reading, planning, drafting and checking as an integrated workflow rather than as
isolated one-off actions.

5 A novel, dual-component pedagogical system for responsible AI
integration

Grounded in the literature review and the mixed-methods findings, this section intro-
duces a dual-component pedagogical system for responsible AI-supported assess-
ment. The instructor-facing AI Engagement Spectrum applies constructive alignment
to set task-specific rules for permissible AI use, while the student-facing AI Scaffold
offers a UDL-aligned workflow that guides students with dyslexia through reading,
planning, drafting and revising with AI support. Together, these components respond
to RQ3 by linking institutional policy, pedagogical intent and day-to-day academic
work, so that decisions about AI use become visible as study activities and process
artefacts within a single AI-aware assessment system rather than an add-on policy
layer.

5.1 The AI Engagement Spectrum and establishing permissible use

Before students apply the AI Scaffold to an assignment, the lecturer must define
clear rules of engagement for that task. This professional judgement is guided by
the Intended Learning Outcomes (ILOs): which skills must students demonstrate
unaided, and where can AI legitimately scaffold learning without undermining those
goals? The AI Engagement Spectrum supports this decision by grouping AI assis-
tance into five use cases that can be stated directly in the assignment brief.
   Within constructive alignment, the Engagement Spectrum and the Scaffold link
outcomes, activities and assessment. ILOs specify the cognitive behaviours a task
should elicit (for example explain, analyse or create); the Engagement Spectrum
turns these verbs into rules about which AI functions may support but not replace
those behaviours, and the AI Scaffold organises students’ study activities so that they
practise the intended skills while generating process evidence alongside the final
product. This makes the rationale for permitting or constraining particular AI uses
explicit to staff and students.

5.1.1 The theoretical foundation of the five AI engagement use cases

The five use cases of the AI Engagement Spectrum (Idea Generation, Research,
Structuring, Drafting and Proofreading) draw on process models of writing. Flower
and Hayes (1981) conceptualise writing as a non-linear cycle of planning, translating
and reviewing, while process-writing approaches distinguish pre-writing, drafting,
revising and editing. Idea Generation, Research and Structuring align with planning
stages; Drafting with translating ideas into text; and Proofreading with reviewing
and editing. This non-linear view justifies the flexible, cyclical structure of the AI
Scaffold, which allows students to move between stages rather than follow a rigid
checklist.

5.1.2 Academic perspective on the five AI engagement use cases

The lecturer must decide which AI functions would scaffold learning versus which
would perform the core cognitive task for the student, risking the assessment’s valid-
ity (Stokkink, 2025). To help lecturers make these informed decisions, the spectrum
is organised around five common use cases identified in Section 5.1.1, each repre-
senting a key stage in the academic writing process. The following subsections detail
each of these. For each one, we will first define its specific Function, describing how
AI can be used at that stage, and then outline the Lecturer’s Consideration, which
provides the pedagogical rationale for either permitting or restricting its use based on
the assignment’s intended learning outcomes.

Use Case 1: AI for idea generation and brainstorming Function: Using AI as a cre-
ative partner to brainstorm topics, overcome writer’s block, formulate initial ques-
tions, or explore potential arguments.

   Lecturer’s Consideration: This is often permitted as it supports the initial, diver-
gent thinking phase (a key part of Bloom’s create level) of writing without substitut-
ing for the core analytical work to follow, unless independent idea innovation skills
are a core component in the assessment.

Use Case 2: AI for research Function: Using AI to identify relevant search terms,
find potential academic sources, or generate summaries of dense articles to aid
comprehension.

   Lecturer’s Consideration: Permissible when the ILO focuses on synthesis
or application (Bloom’s apply or analyse), but may be restricted if the ILO is to
develop the skill of literature searching itself (which relates to Bloom’s remember
and understand).

Use Case 3: AI for structuring and outlining Function: Using AI to organise brain-
stormed ideas or research notes into a coherent outline or structure for an essay or
report.

This pathway is designed to help students manage and understand source material,
directly addressing the significant challenges faced by learners with dyslexia in text-
heavy academic environments.

5.3.1 Providing multiple means of representation

Academic success often depends on processing large volumes of dense text, which
can be particularly challenging for students with dyslexia because decoding is slow,
fatiguing and cognitively demanding. The RSP offers two main interventions that
provide multiple means of representation, a core UDL principle.
   First, the framework supports the use of AI-powered summarisation. This inter-
vention directly addresses the challenge of cognitive load by condensing lengthy
articles or book chapters into digestible key points. This helps students focus their
limited working memory resources on comprehension and analysis rather than on
initial processing. The high demand for this function is confirmed by the survey data
indicating that summarising texts is the single most common use of AI among stu-
dents, reported by 65% of respondents.
   Second, it recommends the use of TTS technology, which uses auditory process-
ing to bypass the phonological decoding barrier that is a hallmark of dyslexia. This
allows students to access information through listening, which can be a more efficient
and less cognitively taxing channel. Together with summarisation, this supports both
perception and building knowledge by highlighting patterns and critical features. The
recommended workflows for both TTS and AI-powered summarisation are visual-
ised in Fig. 6a and b, respectively. When summarisation is permitted under the AI
Engagement Spectrum, students should treat it as a comprehension aid, not a replace-
ment for reading, and proceed directly to the accuracy, and coverage checkpoint.

6.1 The nested scaffolding model as a transferable architecture for responsible
AI integration

The framework is best understood as part of a multi-level system of Nested Scaf-
folding rather than as a single tool. This model offers a transferable architecture for
responsible AI integration with four interconnected layers: institutional, pedagogical,
procedural and cognitive.

1. Institutional scaffold: University-level policies on academic integrity, data pri-
   vacy and ethical technology use set broad principles such as transparency and
   attribution (Cornell University Center for Teaching Innovation, 2023).
2. Pedagogical scaffold: Lecturers interpret these principles in light of constructive
   alignment, analysing ILOs and defining the permissible scope of AI use for each
   assessment (Biggs, 1996; Stokkink, 2025).
3. Procedural scaffold: The AI Scaffold operates at this level, giving students a step-
   by-step workflow for responsible AI use within those task-specific boundaries.
4. Cognitive scaffold: AI tools provide targeted support for sub-tasks such as sum-
   marisation or brainstorming, reducing extraneous load while leaving core rea-
   soning and authorship with the learner (CAST, 2024).

Taken together, these layers show how institutions, academics and students can coor-
dinate AI integration so that it remains ethical, transparent and aligned with core
learning objectives.

6.2 Synthesising flexibility and constraint in AI pedagogy with UDL and
constructive alignment

A key theoretical contribution is the synthesis of UDL and constructive alignment
in AI-assisted pedagogy. UDL provides the imperative to offer flexible routes into
learning and multiple ways of engaging with and expressing understanding (CAST,
2024), while critics warn that it can be interpreted as a licence to remove all challenge
or desirable difficulty (Bjork, 1994; Boysen, 2024; de Bruin, 2023). GenAI intensi-
fies this tension because it can both lower access barriers and displace core cognitive
work.
   Constructive alignment helps to resolve this by tying AI permissions to the cogni-
tive verbs in the ILOs (for example analyse, evaluate or create) (Anderson & Krath-
wohl, 2001; Biggs, 1996). This logic underpins the AI Engagement Spectrum: it
supports flexibility where AI can legitimately scaffold access and expression, and
constraint where AI would replace the skills an assessment is intended to measure.
Table 4 offers a practical heuristic for applying this principle, while the UDL map-
ping in Table 5 in Appendix C shows that the framework as a whole remains system-
atically aligned with UDL 3.0 rather than being an ad hoc collection of tips.

The framework reframes GenAI tools as forms of “assistive intelligence” rather than
simple assistive technologies. Traditional tools such as TTS mainly remove surface-
level barriers in accessing or producing text, whereas GenAI can generate ideas,
outlines and prose. Without guidance, this risks bypassing the cognitive work that

6.4 Practical implications

For SSWS, the framework offers a concrete, dyslexia-aware resource that can be
adapted into workshops, study guides and one-to-one appointments. Table 3 can be
shared with students as a quick reference that links common challenges to suggested
AI use, human checkpoints and UDL principles.
    For lecturers and programme leaders, the AI Engagement Spectrum provides a
structured way to state expectations about AI in module handbooks and assessment
briefs, helping to reduce ambiguity and inconsistency across a programme. Used at
examination boards or curriculum review meetings, it can also surface where assess-
ments rely heavily on product-only evidence and where additional process artefacts
may be needed. As a worked example, Table 4 presents a constructively aligned
“Engagement Spectrum” checklist that links intended learning outcomes, typical
assessment tasks, required process evidence and AI permission levels, which lectur-
ers can adapt directly into module handbooks and assignment briefs.
    At institutional level, the Nested Scaffolding Model suggests how high-level AI
and academic integrity policies, task-level decisions and students’ day-to-day study
habits can be aligned. Rather than issuing generic warnings or permissions, univer-
sities can support departments in adopting locally appropriate AI policies that are
transparent, inclusive and practically implementable.

6.5 Limitations and future work

This study has several limitations. The empirical strand involved a small number of spe-
cialist staff and a modest, self-selecting student sample from a single UK institution, so
the findings cannot be assumed to represent all students with dyslexia or all disciplinary
contexts. We have not yet carried out a full implementation study of the framework or
measured impacts on attainment, workload or perceptions of academic integrity.
   Future work will therefore focus on piloting the AI Engagement Spectrum and AI
Scaffold in specific modules, collecting quantitative and qualitative data on usage
patterns, learning outcomes, and staff and student experiences. Comparative work
with other groups of neurodivergent learners, and with students without diagnosed
learning differences, would help to test the generalisability of the Nested Scaffolding
Model. Finally, as institutional AI policies and tools continue to evolve, there is a
need for longitudinal research that tracks how permissions, practices and expecta-
tions around AI-supported study change over time.

7 Conclusion

This paper has presented the AI Engagement Spectrum and AI Scaffold as a dual-
component, UDL-aligned system for integrating GenAI into the academic work of
students with dyslexia. Developed from mixed-methods data with students and the
SSWS, and grounded in constructive alignment, the framework offers a structured,
tool-agnostic workflow that supports reading, writing and planning while embed-
ding human-in-the-loop checkpoints. In doing so, it moves beyond individual tools to
propose a transferable architecture for AI-supported assessment in higher education.
    The framework’s key contribution is to show how AI can be used to reduce extrane-
ous cognitive load without erasing desirable difficulties, preserving students’ author-
ship and critical engagement. By pairing an instructor-facing permission model with
a student-facing workflow, the Nested Scaffolding approach gives institutions, lectur-
ers and support services a shared language for setting AI expectations and designing
accessible tasks. Rather than framing AI as either a threat or a panacea, the model
illustrates how carefully bounded AI use can enhance autonomy and equity for stu-
dents with dyslexia and profile-similar learners.
    As an exploratory study with a small, context-specific sample, the work reported
here is a starting point rather than a definitive solution. Looking ahead, future research
can examine how the framework functions in larger and more diverse cohorts, and in
disciplines beyond computing. It will also be important to investigate how sustained use
of the AI Scaffold shapes students’ confidence, self-advocacy and attainment, and how
the AI Engagement Spectrum can be embedded within programme-level AI and assess-
ment strategies. Such work would help institutions to translate rapidly evolving AI tech-
nologies into day-to-day practices that are both inclusive and pedagogically robust.
