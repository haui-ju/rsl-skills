## Abstract
<a id="abstract"></a>

s.oyelere@exeter.ac.uk; solomon. This pilot study investigated the potential of integrating Augmented Reality (AR) oyelere@ltu.se and Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) to support engagement and task Full list of author information is management for university students diagnosed with Attention Deficit Hyperactivity available at the end of the article Disorder (ADHD). Using a Design-Based Research (DBR) approach, we evaluated ADHDvance LearnAR across three within-subject conditions (baseline tools, AR- enhanced, and AR+GenAI) with n = 15 undergraduate participants. Quantitative measures showed directional improvements in engagement duration, interaction frequency, and task completion across conditions; however, differences did not reach statistical significance, consistent with the study’s exploratory scale. Qualitative analysis provided explanatory depth, identifying themes related to (i) increased task salience through spatial AR visualisation, (ii) improved task initiation and planning support, and (iii) reduced perceived cognitive load through just-in-time GenAI scaffolding. Findings suggest AR+GenAI may offer promising affordances for inclusive learning design, warranting longitudinal evaluation with larger samples and stronger causal designs.

                  2 Related work
                  2.1 ADHD in higher education and traditional educational limitations
                  ADHD is a prevalent neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by persistent patterns
                  of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, significantly affecting students’ academic
                  and organisational skills in higher education environments. The prevalence of ADHD
                  among university students is estimated to range between 5% and 10%, highlighting the
                  importance of adequately addressing the educational needs of this substantial student
                  population [10]. University students with ADHD frequently struggle with tasks that
                  demand sustained attention, effective time management, and structured organisation,
                  presenting notable barriers to their academic success and personal development [11, 12].
                     The cognitive and behavioural profiles associated with ADHD often lead to chal-
                  lenges within traditional educational settings, particularly those characterised by pas-
                  sive learning methodologies [13]. Common instructional methods in higher education,
                  such as large-group lectures, conventional reading assignments, and standardised test-
                  ing, are typically designed without consideration for diverse cognitive needs [14]. Such
                  approaches require prolonged attention spans, robust executive functioning skills, and
                  consistent self-regulation, areas where ADHD students often experience substantial dif-
                  ficulties. Consequently, these conventional teaching methods can inadvertently exacer-
                  bate issues related to distractibility, reduced motivation, and frustration, culminating in
                  suboptimal academic outcomes for students with ADHD. Research indicates that stu-
                  dents with ADHD consistently demonstrate lower academic performance compared to
                  their neurotypical peers, experiencing higher dropout rates and reduced levels of aca-
                  demic achievement [15–17]. Traditional educational strategies predominantly adopt a
                  one-size-fits-all approach, offering limited flexibility and minimal opportunities for per-
                  sonalisation. This rigid structure poses particular challenges for ADHD students, whose
                  cognitive processing and attentional capabilities fluctuate significantly, even within short
                  timeframes.
                     Furthermore, standard organisational and study tools commonly provided by educa-
                  tional institutions, such as paper planners, basic digital calendars, and noninteractive
                  e-learning modules, are often inadequate for addressing the specific needs of ADHD stu-
                  dents [18, 19]. These tools typically lack the interactive, engaging, and adaptive features
                  essential for maintaining the attention and motivation of ADHD learners. Consequently,
                  the utilisation of conventional organisational resources often leads to increased cogni-
                  tive load, frustration, and decreased overall academic performance among students with
                  ADHD.
                     The limitations of traditional educational methods highlight an urgent need for inno-
                  vative approaches that accommodate diverse cognitive profiles [20, 21]. Educational
                  interventions that leverage interactive and personalised methodologies are essential
                  for effectively supporting students with ADHD. Recent studies underline the effective-
                  ness of active learning strategies that engage students in dynamic, participatory activi-
                  ties rather than passive information consumption [22–24]. These strategies facilitate

                  3 ADHDvance learnAR system design and implementation
                  3.1 System design and implementation
                  3.1.1 Design principles and user-centred rationale
                  ADHDvance LearnAR was developed using a user-centred design (UCD) approach
                  that explicitly aligns system features with the cognitive and behavioural characteristics
                  commonly associated with ADHD. Prior research highlights that students with ADHD
                  often experience executive dysfunction (e.g., planning, working memory, task initiation),
                  heightened distractibility, and fluctuating attention. In response, the design prioritised
                  three guiding principles: simplicity (to minimise cognitive load and reduce distraction),
                  interactivity (to maintain attention through active engagement), and personalisation (to
                  support diverse needs and changing attention states across tasks and contexts).
                    User involvement was embedded throughout the development process through itera-
                  tive design cycles rather than confined to end-stage evaluation. Students with ADHD
                  contributed practical feedback on which features were most useful, where friction
                  occurred, and what interface elements felt distracting or overwhelming. This input
                  shaped key decisions such as reducing visual clutter, ensuring predictable navigation,
                  and limiting the number of steps required to create tasks, trigger reminders, or access
                  support tools. Educators also participated by advising on classroom feasibility, workload
                  implications, and alignment with established study-skills and support practices in higher
                  education.
                    These participatory inputs directly informed the system’s final design: a minimalist
                  interface intended to reduce distraction; short, actionable prompts to support task ini-
                  tiation and reduce decision fatigue; and modular feature access to allow learners to use
                  only the supports they need at a given moment. Together, these design principles and
                  stakeholder contributions ensured that ADHDvance LearnAR was not only technically
                  functional, but also pedagogically appropriate, cognitively accessible, and grounded in
                  the lived experiences of its intended users.

                  The integration of Generative AI within ADHDvance LearnAR enables highly adaptive
                  and responsive educational experiences. Through tools such as the AI Summariser and
                  the ADHDvance Chatbot, the system delivers real-time personalised assistance, includ-
                  ing content simplification, study recommendations, and organisation prompts. These
                  tools respond to students’ behavioural patterns, learning preferences, and prior inter-

                  adaptive components are particularly valuable in helping students sustain momentum

                  the cognitive and behavioural characteristics of students with ADHD. Individuals with
                  ADHD often experience heightened distractibility and sensory sensitivity, making visual
                  simplicity and predictability essential for maintaining focus. To reduce cognitive load,
                  the interface avoids unnecessary animations, dense text blocks, and excessive visual
                  clutter. Consistent iconography, limited colour palettes, and straightforward navigation
                  paths support goal-directed behaviour and reduce the demands on working memory
                  and attention-switching. Feedback from ADHD users during prototyping phases con-
                  firmed the value of clean layouts, chunked information, and simple interactive elements.
                  These design decisions reflect established accessibility guidelines for neurodiverse learn-
                  ers and ensure that the technology supports, not overwhelms, students with attentional
                  and executive function challenges.

                  5.2 Research design
                  The study adopted a within-subjects experimental design comprising three distinct

                  LearnAR application. Initially, participants experienced a baseline condition where they

                  minimise external distractions. The use of standardised instructions and identical timing
                  across sessions further ensured internal validity.

                  7.1 RQ 1: How do AR visualisations and interactive features influence engagement and
                  task completion among university students with ADHD?
                  Across the pilot conditions, AR visualisations were associated with directionally positive
                  patterns in engagement and task performance, although inferential tests did not detect
                  statistically significant differences. The descriptive shifts nonetheless suggest that AR
                  may be a promising mechanism for supporting ADHD learners in higher education, par-
                  ticularly when engagement is operationalised through sustained session time, interac-
                  tion frequency, and completion behaviour. Rather than implying definitive effectiveness,
                  these outcomes indicate feasibility and warrant cautious interpretation consistent with
                  an exploratory DBR evaluation.

                  may lie in how it externalises otherwise fragile executive-function processes. In par-
                  ticular, the qualitative data indicate that AR increased task salience and reduced the
                  “out of sight, out of mind” effect typical of ADHD-related executive dysfunction. Par-
                  ticipants described AR tasks as persistently visible in their workspace and therefore
                  harder to dismiss or forget, effectively offloading working-memory demands and mak-
                  ing deadlines feel immediate. This mechanism-oriented interpretation aligns with prior

                  GenAI less as an “answer engine” and more as a non-judgemental guide that reduced
                  decision fatigue, an interpretation that supports a pedagogically responsible framing of
                  GenAI as scaffolding rather than substitution.

                  The qualitative findings offer the strongest explanatory evidence for how the combined

                  the perceived mental effort required to process dense materials. These accounts align
                  with literature emphasising the role of multimodal and interactive supports in reducing
                  cognitive load for learners with attentional challenges [49], and with perspectives that
                  structured, technology-mediated learning environments can provide targeted cognitive
                  scaffolding [41].
                    In particular, participants described the focus-timer workflow as supporting task ini-
                  tiation and re-entry after breaks, two well-known friction points for ADHD learners.

                  research indicating that interventions targeting organisation, study strategies, and cog-

                  stream impacts beyond immediate engagement measures.

                  longitudinal follow-up or objective measurement of longer-term academic and social
                  outcomes. As such, claims about sustained effects must remain carefully bounded.
                  Future work should directly examine whether the mechanisms observed here (enhanced
                  task salience, improved initiation, reduced cognitive load) translate into durable gains in
                  academic performance, retention, wellbeing, and social functioning over a semester or
                  academic year.

                  process rather than answer production. Short prompts that help students plan, sum-
                  marise readings, or generate study checklists can support executive functioning while

                  7.6 Alternative explanations, limitations, and contrasting evidence
                  Despite the promising patterns, alternative explanations must be considered. First,
                  novelty effects may partly account for increased engagement when participants inter-
                  acted with unfamiliar technology in a short-term setting. Second, structured session
                  conditions and researcher presence may have increased accountability and reduced
                  distractions independent of the intervention. Third, even with procedural controls,
                  within-subject sequencing can introduce learning or carryover effects, complicating
                  attribution.
                    Methodologically, the pilot scale (n = 15) limits statistical power and generalisability.
                  The absence of a comparison condition that excludes all technology-based supports also
                  restricts the ability to isolate intervention-specific effects. These limitations mean that
                  the contribution of this work is best framed as a pilot demonstration of feasibility and
                  design plausibility supported by mixed-method evidence, rather than a definitive evalu-
                  ation of impact.
                    The findings also need to be situated within a wider evidence base that includes mixed
                  outcomes. While many studies report AR’s capacity to increase engagement and moti-
                  vation [47, 49], meta-analytic evidence indicates that AR can increase cognitive load
                  under certain conditions, particularly when tasks are complex or when visual informa-
                  tion becomes distracting [5]. Similarly, while GenAI scaffolds can support learning and
                  organisation, concerns remain about learner dependency and reduced autonomy if scaf-
                  folding becomes overly directive or replaces sense-making processes [9]. These perspec-
                  tives underline the importance of contextual sensitivity, clear pedagogical framing, and
                  responsible design choices (e.g., minimalist UI, short prompts, and modular access) that
                  reduce overload and preserve learner agency.

                  7.7 Implications for inclusive learning design and future research
                  This pilot DBR evaluation suggests that integrating AR and GenAI can offer comple-
                  mentary support for ADHD-related challenges in higher education, particularly in task
                  salience, initiation, and perceived cognitive load. Crucially, the findings point to mecha-
                  nisms rather than generic claims: AR appeared to support externalisation of tasks and
                  deadlines in ways participants found harder to ignore, while GenAI provided just-in-
                  time scaffolding that reduced uncertainty about how to start or how to break tasks into
                  manageable components. For ADHD learners, support for “what do I do next?” may be
                  as consequential as support for attention itself.
                    A key implication for inclusive learning design is that effectiveness may depend less
                  on technological sophistication and more on interaction shape and cognitive acces-
                  sibility. Participants valued simple interfaces, predictable workflows, and short action-
                  able prompts, supporting a “minimum necessary complexity” principle for neurodiverse
                  learners, where reducing friction and cognitive overhead is a primary design objec-
                  tive. The study also suggests AR and GenAI may be most beneficial when positioned as

                  8 Conclusion and future work
                  As a pilot study with a modest sample, the findings primarily demonstrate feasibility
                  and illuminate plausible mechanisms of support rather than providing generalisable evi-
                  dence of effectiveness. We introduced ADHDvance LearnAR, a user-centred application
                  that integrates Augmented Reality and Generative AI to support undergraduate students
                  diagnosed with ADHD, and evaluated it through a Design-Based Research approach.
                  Although quantitative differences across conditions did not reach statistical significance,
                  the consistent directional trends, together with detailed qualitative accounts, suggest
                  that AR may strengthen task salience and sustained engagement through externalisation,
                  while GenAI may reduce planning friction and cognitive load through just-in-time scaf-
                  folding and simplification.
                    The study therefore contributes evidence that the value of these technologies for
                  ADHD learners may lie in how they shape interaction and reduce executive demands:
                  making tasks harder to dismiss, supporting initiation and re-entry, and lowering the
                  effort required to interpret dense information. At the same time, the limited sample and
                  short exposure period constrain inference and emphasise the need to treat the outcomes
                  as exploratory.
                    Future work should prioritise longitudinal, larger-scale evaluations in authentic course
                  contexts, using stronger comparison designs to examine sustained academic, wellbeing,
                  and social outcomes. Further investigation is also warranted into which design elements
                  drive benefit (e.g., adaptive feedback, multimodal representations, or carefully bounded
                  gamification), alongside ethical and equity considerations for GenAI deployment in
                  inclusive education. Overall, this work supports the development of human-centred,
                  cognitively accessible educational technologies that can be tailored to the diverse needs
                  of neurodiverse learners.
