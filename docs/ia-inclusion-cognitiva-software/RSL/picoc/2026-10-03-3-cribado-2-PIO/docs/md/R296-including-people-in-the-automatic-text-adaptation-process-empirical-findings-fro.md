Individuals with intellectual disability (ID) often face significant barriers to participation in society, includ-
ing challenges in accessing written information. These difficulties can contribute to broader exclusion,
particularly in the transition from school to adulthood. In Sweden, 24% of students graduating from upper
secondary school for students with ID were classified as Not in Employment, Education, or Daily activities
(NEED) [1]. A follow-up study highlighted that problems navigating formal support systems played a cen-
tral role in these outcomes [2]. Making written information more accessible is, therefore, an important
step toward greater inclusion. This study investigated the potential of Automatic Text Adaptation (ATA) to
generate Easy Language texts tailored to the needs of secondary school students with ID.

bination with difficulties with social and adaptive abilities [3]. Persons with ID exhibit delays in several

Easy language
Easy Language guidelines aim to make written content accessible to people with cognitive disabilities or
limited reading skills. Crossley et al. [19] highlighted the importance of distinguishing between two levels
of readability: micro-level readability, which concerns the clarity of individual words and sentences, and
macro-level readability, which involves the coherence and overall structure of the text. Easy Language
guidelines address both of these levels, but tension can arise between them—for example, simplifying
sentences at the micro level can inadvertently reduce coherence at the macro level [19].
   Easy Language guidelines operate across: word, sentence, and text/discourse levels [20–23]. At the
word level, the use of common, familiar vocabulary is recommended, while abstract or complex terms
should be avoided. Sentence-level adaptations focus on syntactic simplification such as making sen-
tences shorter, more direct, and clearer. This includes discouraging the use of passive constructions and
nominalizations to ensure it is always evident who is doing what. Adaptations at the word and sentence
level influence the micro-level readability of the text.
   Macro-level readability is influenced by adaptation at the discourse level. Easy language guidelines
emphasize logical organization of content and clear presentation of key information. This may include
strategies such as placing important content early, using headings, and visually highlighting critical
points. Easy Language guidelines are commonly used in many contexts; however, as pointed out by
Wengelin [24], the empirical grounding of such guidelines is scarce. Moreover, the guidelines often
describe Easy Language as a single feature, disregarding the heterogeneity of the reader groups that
may benefit from Easy Language text.
   Automatic Text Adaptation (ATA) refers to the use of computer programs often using AI to automati-
cally simplify texts, making them more accessible and readable. The ATA systems typically rely on
rule-based methods or statistical models trained on examples of simplified texts. In contrast, manual text
simplification is carried out by humans who follow general guidelines but also draw on their own judg-
ment, taking into account factors such as the reader’s background, purpose, and context. While manual
simplification tends to be more flexible and context-sensitive, ATA offers the advantages of lower cost
and scalability, making it possible to apply adaptations to virtually any text [25].
   Techniques that can be used for this purpose are word simplification, sentence simplification, and
summarization, but they also include other techniques such as identifying and explaining keywords,
spelling out abbreviations, or changing the typography of the text to make it clearer and more accessi-
ble to the reader. ATA is today primariy data driven which means that current approaches to text adap-
tation rely mainly on large collections of real-world text data rather than manually crafted rules. To
evaluate the quality and characteristics of automatic adaptations, a set of standard metrics is commonly
used: BLEU [26], SARI [27], and SAMSA [28]. These measures assess quality in terms of semantic similarity
between the adapted and the original text, grammatical correctness, and the degree of textual modifi-

Text adaptations for individuals with intellectual disability
A few studies have explored the effect of Easy Language materials on persons with ID. Fajardo et al. [10]
found that participants with ID gave a high percentage of correct answers to comprehension questions
of Easy Language news articles. Similarly, Karreman et al. [29] found that language adaptations of a
website, following Easy Language guidelines, proved useful to improve comprehension for adults with ID
compared to an original version. Säuberli et al. [30] found that comprehension of manually adapted texts
was better compared to original texts in participants with ID. However, automatically adapted texts did
not help the participants. The authors suggested that this pattern may be due to errors in the ATA ver-
sions and a better alignment between the questions and the original or manually adapted texts com-
pared to the automatically adapted ones.
   However, studies have also failed to find effects of text adaptations for individuals with ID. Adding
connectives and substituting rare words with more frequent ones [31], adding symbols [32], or pictures
[33] are examples of unsuccessful adaptations. One small-scale Swedish study found that some Easy
Language texts provided by public authorities were difficult to read for persons with ID [34], as the
content was too abstract, given the capacity of the readers. The same pattern of results on Easy Language
versions of authority texts was found in a study by Frondén and Kaakinen [35]. Several researchers have
questioned whether conventional guidelines for Easy Language are sufficient for participants with ID,
indicating a need for further investigation into this area [10,24].


Present study
This exploratory study aimed to evaluate how students with ID read and understood ATA texts compared
to the original versions. We used an iterative approach, including several assessments, to gain as much
information as possible about the benefits and challenges of ATA texts. The study was carried out in
three student groups, recruiting nine participants for each iteration. The idea was to improve the adap-
tations from one iteration to the next, based on previous results. The following research questions were
addressed: 1) To what extent do different methods of ATA aid comprehension in students with ID? 2)
How do students evaluate the original and adapted texts with regard to interest, perceived reading
comprehension, and level of difficulty? 3) How do students describe their experiences and challenges
after having read the ATA and original texts? 4) How can different types of assessments inform future
text adaptations for students with ID?


Method
Participants
In Sweden, students with an ID diagnosis are allowed to attend a compulsory school for students with
ID. We contacted five schools and teachers working with student groups with mild to moderate intellec-
tual disability who were deemed able to engage with adapted versions of the national curriculum.
Teachers were asked to distribute consent forms to students in grades 7–9 who they considered capable
of reading simple paragraphs. Each iteration included nine students, with a total of 27 students partici-
pating in the study (11 girls in total, with 1, 5, and 5 girls in each respective iteration). The participants
were between 13 and 17 years old. The mean ages for the three iterations were 15.47 (SD = 0.96), 14.47
(SD = 1.32), and 15.03 (SD = 0.59), respectively. The mean value for iteration 3 is based on only five par-
ticipants due to missing data. The project was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (Dnr:
2021-02918).

Reading comprehension tests
Four texts with five questions each were used to examine reading comprehension of original and adapted
texts in the target group. The texts were taken from age-appropriate domestic science and geography
textbooks and covered different subject areas, but the content was concrete and considered to be famil-
iar to the students (food, health, and countries). Specifically, the texts were about legumes, exercise [40],
Brazil, and Australia [41]. The texts were between 200 and 250 words. Original texts translated into
English are included in Supplementary Appendix 1.
   The texts were presented using formatting principles suggested by the Swedish Agency for Accessible
Media [23] to make them accessible to poor readers. Garamond font size 14 was used with 3-point spac-
ing between lines, blank rows between paragraphs, wide margins, and no justification of text. In addi-
tion, all pictures were removed as the focus of this study was on the text. Images can give away meaning
or answers, shifting focus from reading to interpreting visuals. This reduces validity and may increase
cognitive load, especially for students with cognitive impairments. Headings and subheadings, when
included, were kept and remained consistent across the different text versions.
   Five multiple-choice questions were created for each text. Two of them required making inferences,
while the remaining three addressed explicitly stated information. Each question had four response alter-
natives. The same questions were used for all three test iterations, with just minor adjustments in alter-
natives, to reflect the specific text adaptations. For instance, if a word was replaced by a synonym in one
version of the text, the corresponding question used that same synonym. This ensured that all students
answered questions that were linguistically aligned with the version of the text they had read. The ques-
tions for the original versions of the texts are included in Supplementary Appendix 1.


Structured oral questionnaire
A structured oral questionnaire was completed after each text reading to gather information about the
participants’ post-reading perceptions of the texts and metacognitive insights. The questionnaire con-
sisted of eight structured questions (Supplementary Appendix 2). The first three questions examined
post-reading perceptions of texts and used a scale of 1–5. The students rated the text’s perceived diffi-
culty (1 = very easy to 5 = very difficult), their self-assessed level of comprehension (1 = nothing to
5 = everything), and their interest in the text (1 = very boring to 5 = very interesting). Students were asked
to elaborate on their choice of rating and provide reasoning behind their choices after each of these
questions. The following five open questions focused on metacognitive aspects of the text, i.e., aspects
that they found challenging, such as words and sentences that were difficult to decode or understand.
For these questions, the students were encouraged to revisit the text to refresh their memory of the
challenging parts and to explain the reasoning behind their answers.

In the first iteration of the study, we used general Easy Language adaptations which are common across

Lexical simplification
The lexical simplification module aimed to find and replace complex words. For the first iteration, words
that did not have an entry in the Swedish dictionary SWEVOC [42], indicating that the word is potentially
outside the base vocabulary for a Swedish speaker, were selected as candidates for synonym replace-
ment. If this word had a synonymous equivalent in the Swedish synonym lexicon SYNLEX [43] that was
more frequently occurring (according to the additional frequency annotations to SYNLEX performed by
Keskisärkkä and Jönsson [44], the word was replaced with the synonym.


Syntactic simplification
We used a set of syntactic simplification rules working at the sentence level. The rule set implemented
in the first iteration is presented in Table 2 and has been previously used in various Swedish text sim-
plification projects [45,46].


Procedure
One member of the research team met with each of the students individually on three occasions. During
the first two sessions, reading comprehension of original and adapted texts was examined. All partici-
pants read two original and two adapted texts each and answered 20 comprehension questions (five per
text). During the first test session, each student read two texts within the same subject area (e.g., domes-
tic science), one in its original version and one adapted. The same procedure was followed in the second
test session, but using texts from a different subject area (e.g., geography). The order and version of the
texts were counterbalanced across participants. According to their preference, the students read the text
aloud or silently, and they answered the reading comprehension questions independently. They were
allowed to go back and look at the text while answering the questions. Following the reading of each
text, the oral questionnaire was administered. The last test session focused on testing cognitive abilities
and language skills.

effect size. The same procedure was used for the ratings regarding the post-reading perception of the

Positive perceptions of the texts
Overall, the students were very positive about the texts, describing them as interesting and easy to fol-
low. They were excited about learning new ideas and praised the clear structure of the texts. During
interviews, they also offered suggestions to enhance comprehensibility.
   The students remarked that having an interest in the subject made the text easier to understand. One
student specifically mentioned that the text on exercise was easier compared to others, such as the one
on legumes, because they were more interested in exercise. The student explained, “None were difficult,
but the one about exercise was easier because I’m interested in it.” This suggests a connection between
personal interest, motivation, and how challenging a text is perceived to be.
   Several students wanted to share something they had learned from the text. The student in the quote
below experienced the reading as enjoyable, as the content gave the student more knowledge. “It was
fun to read, I like facts. Now I know almost everything about Australia. I know that they celebrate
Christmas on the beach.” In the text, the author presents many differences between Sweden and Australia
and states that their warmest period corresponds to the coldest period in Sweden. Hence, you can spend
Christmas at the beach. The student described that they had learned a lot and felt that they knew
almost everything about Australia. The quote indicates that the student felt a sense of satisfaction at
having learned something new.
   Some students highlighted structural aspects when describing texts and why they liked them. They
mentioned headings and sub-headings, but also that it was positive that the text dealt with one thing
at a time. One student stated: “Good with headings and sub-headings so you don’t mix everything up.
They talk about one thing at a time”. The student pointed to both the importance of structure and clarity
in the layout of the text. Headings and sub-headings made the text easier to read and facilitated finding
information. Adhering to a single topic at a time results in a more comprehensible and organized text.
   The students were not only positive regarding the texts. One student suggested marking keywords.
“You can write in black but have certain words that are pink or some other color so it will be easier for
those who have difficulty seeing and for everyone, so it will be easier to see where you are in the text”.
The student expressed a wish for tools that make reading easier and suggested that some words could
be marked with a different color. This would both help students who need it because of visual impair-
ment and help all readers to better find information in the text. This suggests that the student recog-
nized a need to scaffold the reader in identifying the key points of the text.
   Another student’s suggestion was to include pictures to illustrate certain aspects of the text. One
student said: “I would like a picture that shows what different vitamins are like vitamin B and what dif-
ferentiates different vitamins.” Some texts in the textbooks originally included pictures, but these were
removed for this project to emphasize isolated reading. The student’s statement could be seen as an
expression of the need to have more background knowledge. They appeared to want more information
about vitamins, what they are, and how they differ from each other. The text does not explain what
vitamins are.


Limited content in sentences makes it easier to read
When the students were asked to go back through the text retrospectively to find and give examples,
most of the students responded that they did not find any sentences difficult. Only two aspects at the
sentence level were commented on by students: sentence length and the use of commas. Short sen-
tences were preferred by several students. After having read the original version of Legumes, one student
expressed that “the text about legumes should have more periods”. Another student found commas
disturbing when reading, especially if there were several commas in the same sentence. When the stu-
dent read a sentence with six types of legumes listed, she experienced that her reading was disrupted.
The student explained: “There are too many commas. I’d rather have for example, and two examples and
not commas. The sentence gets a little messy. There were too many examples”. One student expressed
this as: “In my head, it was too much, when I read everything got mixed up. I needed to digest the text
and think”. This student’s statement, the dislike of commas and preference for short sentences, may be
an expression of the same phenomenon, namely cognitive overload in trying to process a lot of infor-
mation at the same time.

Difficult words as roadblocks
Word difficulties were commonly highlighted when students explained how easy or difficult the texts
were. The link between word comprehension and perceived difficulty appears to be strong. In the ques-
tionnaire, the students were asked to indicate words that were difficult to read and words that were
difficult to understand. For many students, the distinction was unclear, and they often referred to the
same words when describing both aspects.
    Some students talked about the importance of understanding the context and that the understanding
of an entire text is lower if the text contains difficult words. It seems that the difficult words can cause
the reading flow to stop entirely. “I just focused on the fact that I didn’t know the word ‘hemisphere’.
Then the whole sentence became difficult, and I had to read it again”. The student described that getting
caught up on individual words can disrupt the entire reading process. It appears that it was difficult for
the student to shift focus and work around the difficult words, as the word became a disturbing element.
    The students highlighted many words that they had not heard before and did not know the meaning
of. These words were often subject-specific, and closely linked to what the text was about. The students
also expressed that some of the texts dealt with content that they did not know anything about. As an
answer to the question if any words were difficult to read, one student stated: “Sparse, sparser, sparsely
populated, eucalyptus trees – I had never heard those words”. Some other examples from other texts were
kidney beans, protein source, endorphins, obesity, ungulates, hemisphere, suburb, and inhabitant. As these
words are subject-specific, they are difficult to replace, as there may not be very many true synonyms.
However, sparse was substituted with more scattered in the adapted version. The understanding of the
concepts is closely tied to the subject knowledge of the texts. It appears that the context of the texts
was not clear in all cases, which makes the texts much more demanding for any reader.
    The students also gave examples of reasoning about the meaning of unknown words. This quote
came after the student had read the original text Australia. “I understood the introduction, but then I got
to glest befolkat (Eng: sparsely populated) and it was difficult. Glest (Eng: sparsely) should mean flest
(Eng: the most)”. In the original text, the student encountered a sentence where Australia was compared
to Sweden, as they are both sparsely populated. The student realized that sparsely was an unknown
word and tried to figure out the meaning. Here, they suggested a similarly spelt word that is very dif-
ferent in meaning. For a typical reader, the context described with deserts and dry grasslands, which
makes it impossible to live in those areas, would be clues to figure out the meaning of the word sparse.
However, that information did not help the student. To figure out the meaning of a word by connecting
it to a similarly spelt word is an immature reading comprehension strategy, which suggests a focus on
the word level rather than the text level.
    In Swedish, compound words can be quite long, as they can consist of combinations of long words,
or three or more words written together. Some examples from the used texts were kontorsskyskrapor,
lyxbostäder, tjocktamscancer and eukalyptusträd (Eng: office skyscrapers, luxury homes, colon cancer, and
eucalyptus trees). One student explained that they read too fast and could not decode the words at that
speed. Adapting reading speed to the difficulty of the words, does not seem to be done automatically
for the student. One student commented on the word tjocktarmscancer (Eng: colon cancer), which starts
with the /ɕ/ sound in Swedish: “I know the /ɕ/ sound, but when the words are long, I lose my way, and
I have to read again”. The /ɕ/ sound is a voiceless alveolo-palatal fricative which is an exception to
Swedish spelling rules. The sound can be spelt in several different ways that you must learn by heart.
The student in the example above is familiar with the sound and has most likely practiced the connec-
tion between the spelling and the sound. The student’s response indicated that decoding still hindered
reading comprehension, as the focus remained on spelling conventions during the discussion of the text.
To sum up, at the word level, the students experienced difficulties with unknown specific words, long
words, and irregularly spelled words.

multiple-choice test raised concerns about its reliability for our students. The multiple-choice format,

   The SVT measures literal comprehension through a straightforward yes/no question: “Do you recognize
this content from the text?”. Four types of sentences are presented: (a) original sentences from the text, (b)
meaning-altered sentences where the meaning is changed (e.g., “Monaco is one of the biggest countries”
instead of “smallest”), (c) distractor sentences on the same topic but not in the text, and (d) paraphrased

the surface meaning of the texts [48]. For our participants, we simplified the format by including four orig-
inal sentences, two meaning-altered sentences, and two distractors, eight questions per text. This allowed
for easier instructions and simply asking “Do you recognize this sentence from the text?” without distin-
guishing paraphrases. The test items (sentences) were matched to the text version each student had read.
   The results from iteration 1 indicated that the initial set of adaptations was insufficient, as they did
not enhance students’ reading comprehension of the adapted texts, and word-level difficulties appeared
to hinder comprehension. If comprehension is hindered by word-level difficulties, additional adaptations
are unlikely to yield substantial improvement. For these reasons, we focused on the word level in the
second iteration.


Implemented adaptations iteration 2
First, we replaced SWEVOC with an extended version of NYLLEX [49]. The rationale behind this change
was to be able to exclude more basic words not necessary for replacement, since NYLLEX contains
almost twice the number of entries compared to SWEVOC. Consequently, we could be less restrictive in
the following steps of the replacement process, since the probability that the word considered for a
replacement was a word outside of the base vocabulary was higher.
   Second, we changed the approach to how to select a synonym in SYNLEX. In iteration 1, we selected
the possible synonym with the highest frequency. In iteration 2 we implemented a Swedish Fasttext [50]
word vector model to select the synonym with the highest semantic similarity, from all of the entries in
SYNLEX with a higher frequency than the word currently considered for replacement. This also allowed
us to filter possible synonyms that semantically were too far from the original word according to the
Fasttext model. Taken together, these two changes made it possible for the automatic adaptation tool
to select from a wider range of candidate synonyms, while also providing additional filtering steps to
ensure a higher quality of the suggested synonym.
   Epithets, such as the country Australia (rather than Australia), were added to the text [51]. The epithet
module was able to identify geographical epithets, organizations, and persons, but in the final texts, only
the geographical epithets were identified and added to the adapted texts. This change was motivated
by the fact that some words were still too difficult to understand for the students with ID. To use explan-
atory epithets is an advice given by the Swedish Agency for Accessible Media [23].
   We changed the syntactic simplification framework to use the simplification framework developed by
Priscepov [52]. We made two modifications to the automatic adaptation tools to better align with our
objectives. First, we refined the syntactic simplification rules to minimize errors. Second, we deactivated
one rule, the Time Adverbial, which occasionally introduced errors into the texts. Descriptions of the
texts and adaptations are included in Supplementary Appendices 3 and 4.

Methods iteration 3
Based on the results from iteration 2, we concluded that we had reached the limits of synonym replace-

focus to alternative strategies for addressing challenging vocabulary and introduced two discourse-level
adaptations along with a typographic adaptation to further support comprehension. These changes were
motivated by results from previous iterations, which indicated that vocabulary continued to pose a chal-
lenge for many readers, as reflected in their responses to the metacognitive insight questions. Also, the
overall comprehension, as measured by the multiple-choice questions, remained low. Additionally, stu-
dent feedback revealed a dislike for sentences with multiple commas.

Metacognitive insights
The differences in the participants’ use and perception of evident features of iteration 3 adapted texts
(summary, bullet points, and explained keywords) are outlined in the themes: Positive perception of
text-level adaptations, and Text-level adaptations are insufficient when reading comprehension is low.


Positive perception of text-level adaptations
Most participants answered that they thought the adaptations were good when asked a direct question.
This included the summaries, the highlighted words with explanations, and the bullet lists. Mostly, the
participants did not expand on why these aspects were helpful for them, even when prompted to do
so. It is difficult to know if the features made a difference for the participants based on such statements.
More specific feedback on the features was given by a few participants. Concerning the summary, one
participant stated: “The short version was good, it helped me understand what the text was about”. This
statement is in line with the idea of including summaries to help the participant activate background
knowledge of the text and process it better. The statement does suggest that the participant found it
useful in such a way.
    Regarding the highlighted and explained keywords, many participants considered this a positive fea-
ture, with specific comments noting that “it made the important words in the texts easier to spot”.
Several individuals revisited the explanations, but one participant mentioned that “it was difficult to
remember the explanations.” This highlights the challenge of shifting attention from the text to the spe-
cific word, reading its definition, and then retaining that definition when refocusing on the text.


Text-level adaptations are insufficient when reading comprehension is low
Some participants’ statements indicated that the adaptations were perceived as less helpful than intended.
It was noted that the added length of the text, due to the inclusion of the summary at the beginning,
made it more exhausting to read. This indicates that reading is laborious and keeping it short may be
more important than trying to help comprehension by priming readers to the content.
   A few readers mentioned that they overlooked adapted aspects while reading. Also, one participant
admitted they did not go back to review the provided explanations, even though they stated that the
“black words” were unfamiliar (keywords highlighted in bold). One participant claimed she knew all the
words already, including the bolded ones. All these statements suggest that the adaptations were not
aligned with the reading process of the participants.


Discussion
In this exploratory study, we used Automatic Text Adaptations (ATA) to modify texts at the word, sen-
tence, and discourse to improve reading comprehension for secondary school students with ID. Below,
we address the study’s research questions, focusing on: (a) differences in comprehension scores between
original and adapted versions of the texts; (b) readers’ ratings of interest, perceived reading comprehen-
sion and level of difficulty; (c) the challenges readers encountered while engaging with the texts; and
(d) how the various assessment methods used in the study can inform future text adaptations for stu-
dents with ID.


Comprehension of expository text
The initial adaptation employed general Easy Language guidelines as a starting point and modified syn-
tax and vocabulary. The average scores for multiple-choice questions were very similar between both
versions of the text, which is in line with previous studies showing that there is no guarantee of adapted
texts improving comprehension in readers with ID [31–33]. Although it may seem counterintuitive, there
are several reasons why text adaptations might not always be effective. First, adaptations may benefit
some readers but not others, as this population is highly heterogeneous. This pattern appears to exist in
our study as well, as can be seen in the individual trajectories. One possible explanation is an interaction
between reading comprehension level and the effectiveness of adaptations. Fajardo et al. [58] found that

original texts in the adapted versions but expressed it in simpler terms. Other approaches, such as sum-
marizing and removing content, could potentially have been more effective for some students; however,
this approach carries the risk of oversimplifying the material for those with higher ability levels. A second
explanation why text adaptations may not work for students with ID is that linking text information to
prior knowledge is essential for recalling content but poses a significant challenge for this group of
readers [11], likely a consequence of limited working memory capacity. While lexical and syntactic adap-
tations may improve decoding and comprehension of individual sentences, adaptations beyond the
micro level seem essential to provide the scaffolding needed for comprehension and linking [59].
Examples of macro-level adaptations are strategies to enhance coherence, such as reorganizing text con-
tent or employing rhetorical devices like evocations and refutations to activate readers’ prior topic knowl-
edge [60]. Several studies on ID have found unexpectedly low comprehension levels, especially for
abstract texts requiring expert knowledge [32,35,58]. The number of complicated words that the stu-
dents reported in this study suggests that the text topics may have been challenging for them. Topic
familiarity is crucial for comprehension, especially for struggling readers [61]. The texts used were selected
from age-appropriate textbooks, assuming that students would have some prior knowledge. However,
we may have overestimated their reading abilities in relation to relatively unknown expository topics.
    In iterations 2 and 3, test results across both types of assessments generally indicated improved com-
prehension for the adapted texts compared to the original versions, with one exception. Although the
differences were not statistically significant, we consider the small to moderate effect sizes potentially
meaningful, given the limited sample size of only nine participants per iteration. These results corre-
spond to research findings of positive results on adapted texts for students with ID [10,29,30]. Since
multiple adaptations were applied simultaneously, it is not possible to determine which specific changes
contributed to the improvement. Beginning in iteration 2, the syntactic adaptation tools were enhanced,
resulting in more frequent sentence splitting and adaptations to achieve straight word order compared
to iteration 1. Additionally, improvements to the synonym replacement corpus led to a slightly higher
number of synonym substitutions in some texts. However, a persistent challenge was that many of the
words students found difficult were conceptual terms without true synonyms, making them difficult or
impossible to replace.
    In the third iteration, we kept the adaptations from iteration 2 and focused on discourse-level adap-
tations and word definitions. While students showed better performance on the adapted texts in the
multiple-choice tests, comprehension levels were comparable for both text versions in the SVT test. The
discrepancy between the two reading comprehension test results is difficult to interpret. However, the
findings suggest that surface-level comprehension may not have been supported by the addition of
text-level features such as increased length, bolded keywords, and bullet lists. In addition to being help-
ful, these features could also interrupt reading flow and thereby comprehension. In general, the results
of students taking part in iteration 3 showed higher variability compared to the other student groups,
indicated by higher standard deviations.


Evaluations of text and perceived comprehension
In Iteration 1, we found negligible differences in the reading comprehension measures, but student rat-
ings indicated a preference for the adapted texts. Students perceived them as easier, more interesting,
and felt they understood them better. In Iteration 2, there were measurable differences in reading com-
prehension favouring the adapted texts; however, all ratings suggested a preference for the original ver-
sions. In Iteration 3, the student performed better on the adapted texts on the multiple-choice questions
but rated higher self-assessed comprehension for the original texts along with greater interest in the
adapted versions. These patterns are not in line with what we expected, namely higher self-assessed
comprehension, lower difficulty ratings, and greater interest for the text versions where students demon-
strated better performance. If we focus on the discrepancy between self-assessed reading

reported high levels of comprehension and low levels of difficulty. Overestimation of comprehension was
also observed in a study by Fajardo et al. [58], where students with ID believed they had answered 80%
of the questions correctly, while their actual accuracy was only 55%. It is important to consider what
comprehension means to the participants themselves. While the ultimate goal of reading is understand-
ing, comprehension is not an all-or-nothing process [62] and often depends on the reader’s own criteria.
The interviews indicated that students often felt satisfied by learning a new fact or recalling a specific
detail from the text. Remembering such a fact may have contributed to a positive self-assessment of
their comprehension, even if a deeper understanding was lacking. Furthermore, evaluating the gap
between what there is to understand and what one has understood is a complex metacognitive task.
The format of the comprehension questions may also contribute to a false sense of success, as it only
requires students to select an option or respond with a simple yes or no. Doing so, even without care-
fully considering the alternatives, can create a sense of completion or satisfaction, despite limited actual
understanding.

tially in iteration 3. These ratings may reflect students’ reactions to the somewhat unfamiliar format of
the Easy Language texts. Although few students explicitly expressed this, one noted that the increased
length, due to the addition of a summary, made the adapted text more difficult. Other unfamiliar fea-
tures, such as unusual vocabulary, sentence structures, or formatting, may also have shaped students’
perceptions. The adapted texts might have felt less natural, potentially due to their shorter or more
fragmented sentence construction.


Thinking and talking about the text
When asked about reading challenges, students mainly mentioned unfamiliar words, particularly complex
concepts and compound words. They did not distinguish between decoding difficulties and comprehen-
sion issues and did not verbalize any strategies to infer word meanings from context. At the sentence
level, they disliked long sentences and the frequent use of commas to separate ideas, likely due to dif-
ficulties with working memory. This is similar to challenges seen in individuals with dyslexia, where
decoding is not automatic [64]. Positively, they appreciated headings, subheadings, and text formatting,
which were the same for both original and adapted text versions. Interestingly, none mentioned sen-
tence structures like subordinate or passive clauses, often flagged as difficult in Easy Language Guidelines
[22], perhaps because these features were less noticeable compared to more obvious text elements.
Effective remediation of poor comprehension by the reader requires a triggering event, something that
stands out as unfamiliar or unexpected [62]. The goal of the metacognitive interviews was to collect
triggering events for our target group. When decoding is fluent and understanding is effortless, anoma-
lies are more easily noticed and remembered. However, triggering events are less likely to occur when
overall comprehension is low, as was likely the case for this group of students. This may explain why
participants identified few issues when discussing the text.
    In the third iteration, students expressed positive views on features like summaries and highlighted
keywords, but their responses may reflect acquiescence rather than genuine metacognitive insight, as
few could explain how these features helped. Acquiescence, a tendency to agree regardless of the ques-
tion, is common in individuals with ID [65], and may stem from broader patterns of submissiveness and
compliance, potentially influencing their feedback.
    Effectively using text-level features requires metacognitive abilities and flexibility. While some students
attempted to engage with features like revisiting word definitions, they struggled to retain the informa-
tion due to the challenging concepts in the texts. More critically, they lacked the necessary reading
strategies to benefit from these adaptations. While text-level modifications can be helpful, their effective-
ness diminishes if readers lack the skills to engage with them fully. Combining text-level adaptations

Methods to inform future adaptations
Assessing reading comprehension is critical for evaluating the effectiveness of text adaptations for stu-
dents with ID. Since their reading abilities are often overestimated [58], ensuring that texts are at an
appropriate level and using suitable test formats is essential [68,69]. In this study, the participants scored
quite poorly on the multiple-choice items, with comprehension levels ranging from 36% to 59%. We
wanted to rule out the possibility that the test format itself was too complex for the students and there-
fore did not accurately reflect their true comprehension. To address this, we introduced an additional
task in Iteration 2. In the Sentence Verification Technique (SVT) task, participants were asked whether
they recognized specific sentences from the texts, assessing surface-level understanding compared to the
inferential demands of the multiple-choice questions. Thus, this task simplified both the question com-
plexity and the response format. With this format, students achieved between 63% and 79% correct
answers, suggesting that the SVT task may better align with their actual comprehension levels. However,
it is important to note that both assessment formats are likely influenced by guessing. In the SVT task
in particular, participants have a 50% chance of selecting the correct answer by guessing alone.
    The findings from the reading comprehension tests guided further adaptations at the word and sen-
tence levels in iteration 2. In iteration 3, interview data and limited possibilities to further develop the
synonym function prompted a shift in focus. In retrospect, discussing the thoughts behind the selection
of an answer with the students could have been informative. As we have discussed previously, evoking
metacognitive reasoning is difficult but necessary to understand the thought behind a selection, and
what it says about comprehension and strategies.
    Even though post-reading perception of text ratings may seem ineffective as an outcome measure, as
the participants overestimated their abilities due to weak meta-comprehension, this was important infor-
mation for us in trying to understand comprehension and metacognition of the participants in combi-
nation with the other methods. However, these results did not affect later adaptations.
    The most valuable insights came from the discussions, which revealed difficult words and guided our
word substitution strategy. Interviews highlighted how memory limitations affected comprehension, but
participants did not express clear preferences about sentence structure, so we relied on Easy Language
guidelines [22]. Although the students expressed that the features were positive, we saw limited engage-
ment with discourse-level and word explanation adaptations applied in iteration 3.
    In summary, each assessment presented its own challenges, yet all contributed to a deeper under-
standing of the students’ comprehension. This aligns with the recommendation to use triangulation
when working with this population [70]. Although we gained some insights into improving text adapta-
tions, applying these findings to make the texts easier proved difficult, likely in part due to the initial
overestimation of the students’ reading comprehension when selecting texts.


Limitations
In this study, we conducted extensive testing of text comprehension but did not include standardized
measures of reading comprehension or IQ due to test prioritization. This represents a limitation as it may
reduce the generalizability of the findings. While IQ levels were in the mild to moderate range, a more
precise assessment would likely have provided a valuable context for interpreting individual differences
in reading comprehension. Similarly, the absence of a reading comprehension baseline limits our ability
to determine whether the effects of the adaptations were influenced by reading ability, as suggested in
previous research.
   We examined the effectiveness of different adaptation strategies; however, each iteration involved a
new group of participants, making direct comparisons less reliable. Although the groups were broadly
similar in cognitive, linguistic, and age profiles, one group consisted almost entirely of male participants,
which may have influenced the results.

Conclusions
This exploratory study aimed to evaluate how students with ID read and understood ATA texts com-
pared to the original versions. We used three rounds of testing on new groups of students and
improved text adaptations based on results from previous rounds. We found that syntactic and lexical
adaptations can make a difference in comprehension results, but the effect of adding discourse-level
adaptations and giving word definitions was less clear. The students’ ratings of perceived difficulty,
self-assessed reading comprehension, and interest did not align with their actual reading comprehen-
sion results. This discrepancy may be explained by limited metacognitive skills and a preference for
texts that resemble more typical or familiar formats. While the students mentioned having trouble with
difficult concepts and long sentences, their limited ability to reflect on their understanding made it
hard for them to point out exactly what needed to be changed. Still, talking to students about what
they found difficult was very helpful for guiding further improvements. Overall, the findings suggest
that ATA can be helpful, but students likely need additional support when reading factual or informa-
tional texts.
